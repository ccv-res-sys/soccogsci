How People Understand Other Minds

In making sense of human behavior, people connect the observed with the unobserved—they find meaning in behavior by inferring mental states. This ability is essential for succeeding in the social world: Without mental state inferences, observed behaviors turn blurry and indistinct, future behaviors are difficult to predict, and communicating with others becomes utterly perplexing (Malle, 2002a; Malle & Hodges, 2005).

Mental state inferences are embedded in a broader toolbox of social cognition, which reaches from detecting intentionality to automatic empathy, from imitation to simulation, from goal inferences to personality inferences (Malle, 2008b). Underlying all these psychological processes, however, is a conceptual framework centered on th concept of intentionality (Malle & Knobe, 1997a): human behavior is seen as either intentional or unintentional, and if intentional, then guided by beliefs, desires, and other mental states (Malle, 1999, 2005a). I have proposed that the common terms "folk psychology" or "theory of mind" best denote this conceptual framework that support the broad variety of social-cognitive capacities (Malle, 2004, 2005e). If so, the debate over whether theory of mind consists mainly of "simulation" or "inference" becomes meaningless — theory of mind is a conceptual network within which both simulation and inference processes operate.

In our empirical research we have looked more closely at people’s explicit inferences of mental states in contexts of social interaction (Malle & Pearce, 2001) and social observation (Malle, 2005e). Each time people encounter a behavior, they make many simultaneous inferences —e.g., about goals, beliefs, emotions—and the question becomes how these inferences relate to each other and to other inferences, such as about personality or morality. Previous work on social cognition has typically studied each of these inferences in isolation; in a series of recent experiments we have examined them simultaneously and have discovered a possible hierarchy among them.
