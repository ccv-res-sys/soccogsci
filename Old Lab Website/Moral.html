Free Will and Morality

When people make a moral judgment they evaluate an agent’s behavior in light of a system of norms. Our work focuses on the first part -- the analysis of the agent's behavior and the inevitable inferences about the agent’s mental states before, while, and even after performing the behavior. This analysis of mind and behavior is equally relevant to everyday life (Malle, 2006) as it is to legal decision making (Malle & Nelson, 2003).

We assume that people interpret (im)moral behavior just as they interpret any other behavior; thus, folk psychology lies at the heart of morality (Guglielmo, Monroe, & Malle, 2009). Accordingly, when observing an agent who caused a harmful outcome, people consider first whether the agent acted intentionally or not (Malle, 2006c). This judgment of intentionality (Malle & Knobe, 1997) then branches into further considerations of justification, obligation, and preventability (see Figure 2; Guglielmo et al., 2009; Malle, Guglielmo, & Monroe, in press).

However, some scholars (e.g., Joshua Knobe, Thomas Nadelhoffer) have challenged the assumption that intentionality precedes moral judgments. Instead, they argue that moral judgments actually influence intentionality judgments — that behaviors are more readily considered intentional when they are immoral than when they are morally neutral. In a series of studies we have examined the evidence for this possibility (Guglielmo & Malle, 2010a, 2010b), but our data suggest that under methodological and theoretical scrutiny such influence patterns of morality —> intentionality disappear.

But extant research has focused on verbal judgments in response to carefully constructed stories. In an ongoing set of reaction-time studies we are investigating the temporal order of moral judgments and intentionality judgments in response to a variety of everyday behaviors, displayed verbally or visually (Guglielmo & Malle, in preparation). The data so far suggest that intentionality judgments precede morality judgments.

An additional project concerns the grounding of moral judgment in a conception of "free will." In particular we are examining the hypothesis that mitigation of blame can only occur when an agent's fundamental capacity to choose is in question. This notion of choice underlies both the folk concept of intentionality and the folk concept of free will. This project is described in more detail here.
