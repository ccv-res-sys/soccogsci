Free Will and Morality
What is Free Will to People?
People’s conception of free will is often assumed to be incompatible with a scientific model of the universe. Many scholars describe the folk concept as assuming a special form of nondeterministic causation, or a notion of uncaused causes. However, little research has assessed how people actually conceptualize free will. Our work suggests that people’s concept of free will lacks metaphysical assumptions about causal indeterminism, special causal origin, or the soul. Instead, people define free will quite plainly as a choice that fulfills one’s desires and is free from constraints (Monroe & Malle, 2010). We are currently working on developing and refining this model with the help of a variety of methodologies, including personal interviews, debates, and reaction time studies. Our working hypothesis is that the concept of free will is largely grounded in the concept of intentional agency.

Free Will and Moral Responsibility
Our project also examines how people use their concept of free will to make decisions about the moral and legal standing of other people. For example, we assess how people's beliefs about free will affect their acceptance of various attempts at blame mitigation for transgressions. We are trying to understand which mitigation attempts (e.g., childhood trauma, brain damage, intoxication) succeed and why. If our model of free will is correct then blame mitigation will be successful to the extent that it demonstrates the agent’s limited choice capacity at the time of action. Brain damage or childhood trauma by themselves are not enough; only if they paralyze the capacity to choose can they mitigate blame (Guglielmo, Monroe, & Malle, 2009).

One evolving line of research considers how moral judgments would function in a society that did not believe in free will—a possible future, according to many scholars. We are testing various ways of inviting people to imaginatively put themselves into a world without free choice and—if they can imagine such a world—how they would make moral judgments.

Free Will Itself
Finally, there is the question of whether people really do have anything like free will. In Malle (2006a) I examine various claims about the "illusion" of the will (e.g., Bargh, Libet, Wegner) and conclude that there is nothing in the empirical literature that should make us doubt the adequacy of the ordinary concept of intentional agency—once more, not as a metaphysical concept (which it isn't designed to be) but as the central tool in the social-cognitive toolbox, with which people make sense of mind and behavior.
