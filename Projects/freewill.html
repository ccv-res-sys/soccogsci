Free Will

What is Free Will to People?

People’s conception of free will is often assumed to be incompatible with a scientific model of the universe. Many scholars describe the folk concept as assuming a special form of nondeterministic causation, or a notion of uncaused causes. However, little research has assessed how people actually conceptualize free will. Our work suggests that people’s concept of free will lacks metaphysical assumptions about causal indeterminism, special causal origin, or the soul. Instead, people define free will quite plainly as a choice that fulfills one’s desires and is free from constraints (Monroe & Malle, 2010). We have recently refined this model with the help of a variety of methodologies, including personal interviews, debates, and reaction time studies (Monroe & Malle, in press). The concept of free will really boils down to a concept of free action, which is grounded in the concept of intentionality, with the added provision that the agent not be under heavy constraints. Because people are likely to consider constraints on a continuum, they are also likely to ascribe freedom to actions on a continuum — though this hypothesis has not yet been put to the test.

Free Will and Moral Moral Judgment

Our project also examines how people use their concept of free will to make decisions about the moral and legal standing of other people. For example, we assess how people's beliefs about free will affect their acceptance of various attempts at blame mitigation for transgressions. If our model of free will is correct then blame mitigation will be successful to the extent that it demonstrates the agent’s limited choice capacity at the time of action. Brain damage or childhood trauma by themselves are not enough; only if they specifically paralyze the capacity to choose will they mitigate blame (Guglielmo, Monroe, & Malle, 2009). We have also studied how people make moral judgments about robots, cyborgs, and other creatures that lack either a choice capacity, intentional agency, or a metaphysical capacity such as a "soul." We have found that people are happy to make moral judgments about agents who have no soul, as long as they have choice capacity; but people are not willing to make moral judgments about agents who have no choice capacity, even if they have a soul (Monroe, Dillon, Malle, 2014).

Free Will Itself

Finally, there is the question of whether people really do have anything like free will. In Malle (2006a) I examine various claims about the "illusion" of the will (e.g., Bargh, Libet, Wegner) and conclude that there is nothing in the empirical literature that should make us doubt the adequacy of the ordinary concept of intentional agency — once more, not as a metaphysical concept (which it isn't designed to be) but as the central tool in the social-cognitive toolbox, with which people make sense of mind and behavior. With philosopher Adina Roskies I have also explored the consequences of this position for an understanding of desert and classic questions about responsibility and free will (Roskies & Malle, 2013).
