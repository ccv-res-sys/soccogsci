The Hierarchy of Social Inferences

Whenever they encounter another person’s behavior, people have to process a bewildering amount of information (Malle, 2005e, 2008): What is the person’s gender, age, role? Is the person's behavior intentional? What are her goals, thoughts, and feelings? Does the behavior reveal a part of her personality?

Answers to these questions demand social inferences: conjectures about the other person’s attributes, actions, and mental states. Previous research has provided some insight into these inferences, but they were always studied in separation. In real life, multiple social inferences occur simultaneously within the human perceiver and must therefore be studied simultaneously in research.

Our main goal in this recent project is to discover the order underlying social inferences: How frequently and how fast do people make these inferences? Is there a hierarchy of inferences—a priority by which people infer intentionality, mental states, and personality? What patterns of relationships exist among these inferences? Do some of them compete with one another or speed each other up?

We have developed an experimental paradigm that allows us to study these research questions under controlled condition. Unlike previous research that used written sentences to elicit social inferences, we present videos of human behaviors as dynamic experimental stimuli (click here for the stimulus database). While people make social inferences as they normally would in real life, researchers assess the frequency and exact timing of these inferences.

In our first series of studies we found reliable evidence for a hierarchy of social inferences ranging from intentionality and goals as both the easiest and fastest inference objects, then beliefs being harder and slower, and finally personality as the hardest and slowest of all. We now have a manuscript in press (Malle & Holbrook, 2012), and all means and SDs are available as supplementary material. More work is under way, especially on the mutual facilitation or inhibition of these inference types.

Malle, B. F., & Holbrook, J. (2012). Is there a hierarchy of social inferences? The likelihood and speed of inferring intentionality, mind, and personality. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 102, 661–684.

Malle, B. F. (2008b). The fundamental tools, and possibly universals, of social cognition. In R. Sorrentino and S. Yamaguchi (Eds.), Handbook of motivation and cognition across cultures (pp. 267-296). New York: Elsevier/Academic Press.

Malle, B. F. (2005e). Three puzzles of mindreading. In B. F. Malle & S. D. Hodges (Eds.), Other minds: How humans bridge the divide between self and other (pp. 18-35). New York: Guilford Press.
