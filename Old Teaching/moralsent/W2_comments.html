[Tue Apr 10] Seminar: Classic writings on responsibility and moral sentiments

 1. Aristotle (1955/330B.C.) Conditions of responsibility for action. From The Nicomachean ethics(Trans. J. A. K. Thomson)(Book 3, ch.1, ch. 5). London: Penguin.
      The classic analysis of the folk psychology of intentionality, free will, and responsibility. Has some flaws, but an inevitable starting point. Which are the conditions of responsibility that Aristotle proposes? And which are valid excuses?

2. Hume, David (1998/1751). Concerning moral sentiment. Excerpt from Enquiry concerning the principles of morals (pp. 83-89). Oxford: Clarendon Press.
      In this passage, Hume introduces his analysis of moral judgments as sentiments. This analysis is based on his analysis of causal judgments, which according to Hume are based on associations in the mind (and not necessarily about "real" causal connections between physical bodies). Likewise, moral judgments do not refer to anything "really moral" in the agent judged but to the psychological workings of the mind in the perceivers who forms the judgment. This is a "natural" analysis of morality because it places moral issues in the psychology of the perceivers, amenable to scientific study.

3. Smith, Adam (1976/1759). Excerpts from The theory of moral sentiments. (Edited by D. D. Raphael and A. L. Macfie). Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1976.
Part I, section I (pp. 9-23); Part II, section I (pp. 67-78); Part IV (pp. 179-193).
      Adam Smith followed Humen in studying the human response to moral action (not moral action itself). When he spoke of "theory of moral sentiments" he meant the ordinary "folk theory" that people rely on when they judge the morality of others’ actions. Smith’s basic situation is one involving three people: the spectator, the agent, and the beneficiary of the agent’s action. A central concept in Smith’s analysis is "sympathy" (nowadays usually called "empathy"), the sentiments a spectator has when seeing him- or herself in the situation of another. But this empathy does not emerge for every "passion" (emotion) in the other person–for which ones does it?
      Smith also describes how people arrive at judgments of approval or disapproval. How do they? Not how much emphasis Smith puts on the analysis of the agent’s motives in causing a certain effect in the recipient.
Smith then relates the sentiment of gratitude to actions of reward and the sentiment of resentment to the action of punishment, thus showing how moral sentiments can lead to evaluative actions. But these actions are directed toward the agent, after empathizing with the recipient (and feeling, say, gratitude or resentment).
      In sum, we see that Smith describes the spectator as a complex social perceiver, who empathizes with the recipient, judges the agent based on moral sentiments the spectator feels on behalf of the recipient, and finally acts on these moral sentiments to reward or punish the agent.
