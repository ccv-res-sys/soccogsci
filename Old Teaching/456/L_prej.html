Stereotypes, Prejudice, and Intergroup Conflict

Concepts

Stereotypes: neg/pos sets of beliefs about groups. Stereotypes may sometimes be accurate in the sense of an average group difference but the problematic aspect of stereotypes is to believe that every member of a group has all the features of the average group member (all the features of the stereotype).

Prejudice: negative attitudes towards groups. Prejudice is not only a set of beliefs, but it includes affect. Hate crimes are aggressive acts based on prejudice.

Discrimination: acts of preference for or exclusion of certain groups or group members. Can be based on prejudice or be outside of one's awareness. For example, studies have shown that there is still gender discrimination in college classrooms in the amount of attention and encouragement given to men vs. women. See Prof. Jennifer Freyd's "chilly climate webpage."

Racism, sexism have shades of each of these. I mentioned symbolic (modern/aversive) racism as a special case: concern about being perceived as racist, feelings of prejudice are not expressed or discrimination does not occur unless a "justification" is readily available.

Structure of Stereotypes

Stereotype = category label + attributes as interassociated elements of a mental representation (schema). Example: study by Devine (1989) in which a few subliminally presented attributes, typically associated with the stereotype of African Americans, apparently primed the whole stereotype. Subsequently, subjects who were primed that way interpreted an ambiguous paragraph using an additional attribute from the stereotype (not previously primed), namely, "aggressive."
  • Learned associations (Stangor & Ruble, 1989; study on 4-, 7-, and 10-year-old children and their "congruency bias")

  • Functions of stereotypes:
    1. Categorization. One reason why stereotypes are so powerful and inevitable is because they are part of the human cognitive system. They help us bring order to the flood of perceptual information reaching our mind every split second: they categorize slight variations in the world into more stable categories (colors, shapes, objects, qualities, etc.). That way we can respond to constantly varying circumstances and still use a finite system of categories to make sense of them. This cognitive system, however, fails to fully appreciate the complexity of modern human life, so we often treat the variations among individuals within a group just as we treat incidental variations among tigers within the category "tiger."
      A humorous example of stereotyping can be found here, in which several colleges are reduced to one essential characteristic each.

    2. Simplification. A second function of stereotypes is that they help us simplify information processing. This can be nicely demonstrated when we put people in situations of information overload because they should increase their use of stereotypes in such cases. In fact, Galen Bodenhausen has conducted several studies supporting this reasoning. In one (Bodenhausen, 1990), he compared morning-people and evening-people on an information processing task that they completed either in the morning or in the evening. As expected, morning-people used more stereotypes when they completed the task in the evening, and the reverse was found for evening-people.

Some Origins of Stereotypes (and Prejudice)

  • Mere categorization (see comments above under Functions of stereotypes);
  • Illusory correlation (Chapman & Chapman, 1967; Hamilton, 80's), see below;
  • Classical conditioning (e.g., media presentation of crimes committed by minorities);
  • Social learning, conformity (parents, peers, culture);
  • Power asymmetries (role constraints + fundamental attribution error) and intergroup conflict;
  • In-group bias (ethnocentrism; social identity theory by Tajfel)
  • Authoritarian personality (Adorno, Frenkel-Brunswik)

    Illusory Correlation

    People are very sensitive to the co-occurrence of events, and even more so to the co-occurrence of rare events. For example, we think that we are telepathically gifted if we think of our sweetheart and, seconds later, he or she calls. (Ask yourself: how often have you thought of your sweetheart without the phone ringing within the next few seconds; and how often has your sweetheart called without your thinking seconds earlier...?) Hamilton and colleagues have demonstrated that people tend to overestimate the pairing of rare events (e.g., immigrants or minorities committing crimes) because people pay special attention to those rare pairings. For example, the table below displays fictitious data from a beach, where you normally find more young people than old people and more active behaviors than passive behaviors. In the data as displayed, there is absolutely no relationship (correlation = 0) between being old vs. young and showing active vs. passive behavior. Nevertheless, if we presented the 50 pairings of people and behaviors (each written on a card) in random order, people would tend to overestimate the number of passive behaviors shown by older people - thus, they would see an "illusory correlation." (This effect is not dependent on people holding a stereotype that old people are passive; such a stereotype would, however, increase the strength of the effect.)

Intergroup Conflict

Sherif's (1956) famous "Robber's cave" experiment (see Aronson) demonstrated three important points about intergroup conflict:
  1. even randomly created groups are prone to develop negative stereotypes of each other (mere categorization!);
  2. scarce resources for which the two groups compete most reliably create conflict behavior;
  3. this conflict potential can be reduced when the groups work towards a common goal.
