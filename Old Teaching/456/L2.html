Self-Concept, Self-Regulation, Dissonance

The Self-Concept

The self-concept is a person's representation of him- or herself, the sum of all self-related beliefs, feelings, and memories. Self-representation can be thought of as a projection screen of all self-relevant events that go on inside and outside of a person. Some of what is projected is then stored in memory, and that is what makes up the self-concept.
  • Definition: The self-concept = all beliefs and feelings about oneself that are stored in memory.

  • The self-concept as a whole is hard to study--there are too many different domains. So research focused on identifiable sub-domains, which can be thought of as networks (similar to an associative memory network) or as schemas (see Lecture 1 on attitudes as schemas).

  • Structure: The self-concept has moderate stability on average; domains that are more stable across time can be called the core self-concept, more fleeting domains and elements would be the periphery. Those domains that are stable and whose elements ("nodes") are interconnected in the form of a schema, can be called self-schemas.

  • Complexity: Early work by Nisbett et al. (1973) suggested that people do not like to describe themselves in trait terms -- e.g., "Are you self-controlled or spontaneous?" Rather than saying that they are either self-controlled or spontaneous, they often say that "it depends on the situation." Subsequent research by Sande et al. (1988) found that people do like to describe themselves in trait terms, but in many terms ("I am both self-controlled and spontaneous"), suggesting a greater complexity of the self-concept than of any other person concept (of one's mother, a friend, etc.). Recent results by Locke & Horowitz (1997) clarify that people do like to describe themselves with many trait terms, but only of those terms are at least mildly positive. When presented with trait pairs that are both rather negative, people say they are "neither." Thus, the "complexity" of people's self-concept only holds to the extent that people like to describe themselves in complex positive terms.

  • As mentioned, certain subdomains of the self-concept can contain elements (e.g., beliefs, emotions, memories for a certain domain) that are tightly interconnected. You can think about a schema as an integrated electronic circuit, connecting many lights with each other. Because the lights are interassociated, if you send current to one light, it's likely that other lights will light up as well ("spreading activation"). The more often the lights get activated together, the stronger the connection grows between them (reducing the connection resistance, so to speak).

    The power of schemata is that they can be easily activated with only one or two triggers. For example, I once went through old things and found a perfume bottle that a old girl-friend gave me. I smelled at the bottle and was flooded with the schema of that girl-friend (including feelings, sensations, thoughts, images). A less emotional schema may be that of a professional athlete for his or her sport (including goals, images of past success and failures, beliefs in their ability, workout routines, pain, injuries, etc.)

  • Impact of Self-Schemata on Behavior
    • Quicker response times to stimulus words (e.g., "Extraverted?")
    • More (and more idiosyncratic) examples
    • Greater consistency in self-description
    • Greater confidence in predicting one's own future behavior
    Literature: Markus (1977); Markus et al. (1982, 1985); Deutsch & Mackesy (1985); Malle & Horowitz (1995)

  • Clarification: The difference between self-concept and self-esteem: Self-esteem is a person's global self-regard--how worthy and valuable you think you are on the whole.

Self-Regulation and Bias

  • Some Forms of Self-Enhancement (see Taylor & Brown, 1988):

    Self-serving explanations: insurance protocols, excused for performance failures in sports or academic domains
    Self-serving estimates of own contribution: collaborations, group projects, housework (Ross & Sicoly, 1975)
    Self-serving judgments; in particular:

    The "better than average" bias
    • 90% of business managers report they are "better than the average manager"
    • 86% of workers feel they do a "better than average job performance"
    • Most drivers believe they are safer and more skilled than the average driver
    • Most Americans think they are more intelligent and better looking than the average person
  • Regulation principles: There seems to be an affective motor to feel good about oneself. However, people do not always show self-enhancement. It occurs with great likelihood when costs are low (e.g., nobody can call you on it). But people can also be quite modest and self-critical when costs are high (e.g., when observed by experts or compared to good friend's judgment).

  • Dissonance reduction is one more process to maintain a positive view of oneself

Dissonance Reduction

  • Principles of dissonance theory: two incompatible cognitions --> creates tension --> attempts to reduce dissonance (by changing one, the other, or by integrating both cognitions).

    In the most typical case, a strongly held belief is dissonant with a single behavior. The most interesting cases are those in which dissonance is reduced by changing one's belief. (This is a process in which the usual attitude --> behavior relationship is reversed.)

  • Experiments in the paradigm of forced compliance (self-persuasion): Festinger & Carlsmith (1959) [complete long, boring task, then tell other person that it was fun and interesting; paid $.50, $1, $20]; Cohen (1962) [essay on police force for small or large payment]; Aronson & Carlsmith (1963) [children, toys, mild vs. severe threat]; Zimbardo et al. (1965) [army reservists eating grasshoppers, friendly vs. harsh officer].

    The crucial ingredient in these studies is that all conditions created sufficient force to make people comply with these odd requests, but in some conditions people perceived to have "good reasons" to do so (e.g., severe threat, $10 reward), creating little or no dissonance, whereas in other conditions they had no good reasons (creating high dissonance). The people with high dissonance had to reduce it, and because all other paths were blocked, they changed their attitudes/beliefs (about police, the toy, grasshoppers, etc.) in the direction of their behavior.

  • Aronson describes a number of other paradigms in the chapter (e.g., post-decision, overjustification, effort justification). Despite their difference, what all have in common are the following features:

    (a) behavior appears chosen;
    (b) behavior blatantly violates belief/attitude/self-concept;
    (c) behavior cannot be undone; and
    (d) extraneous justification is blocked. In such a case, a person is likely to rationalize the "violating" behavior by revising a specific belief or attitude so that one's general feeling of self-worth is not harmed. In other words, dissonant situations make a person feel that they acted foolish, irrational, or unjustified, and the dissonance reduction helps avert that feeling.
