Conformity and Obedience

Conformity

The term refers to an individual behavior that follows a real or imagined collective (group, social, cultural) norm or behavior. Conformity surely has its good sides, as in the organization and coordination of groups and societies, but it also has its bad sides, as in the blind obedience to fashions or leaders. Conformity happens to a good extent without deliberation, sometimes without conscious awareness. To minimize our susceptibility to blind obedience, it is a good idea to become aware of our norm-conforming behavior every once in a while and consciously decide to conform (or not to conform), before we return control again to conforming habits, reflexes, and schemas.

General conditions under which conformity is maximized:

  • Ambiguity (in the situation, task, or stimulus)
  • Stress (time pressure, demand, fear, etc.)
  • Others are perceived as experts (or at least as informed as oneself)
  • Need for affiliation (acknowledgment, validation)
The "pitted windshield incidence" illustrates the first and third factors well. The study on the "Invasion from Mars" (a radio show by Orson Wells after H. G. Wells' novel) illustrates especially the first two factors well. The mass suicide of the People's Temple sect in Jonestown illustrates the convergence of all four factors.

The most well-known demonstration of conformity was done by Solomon Asch in the 1950s. His study illustrates the first, second, and third point well.

The Asch Situation

[Asch's stimulus cards and Asch's results]

What makes the situation so uncomfortable? The lack of consensus with others creates "dissonance" on the lowest level: the level of perception.
Two stressful puzzles emerge (which lead to dissonance):

  1. We try to explain the others' behavior, we try to understand how this disagreement is possible. But there is no apparent explanation for their behavior (e.g., no ulterior motive, no outside incentive, etc.).

  2. We wonder how the other people explain our own behavior; we try to anticipate what judgments they make of us, given that we disagree with them. But we cannot think of any reasonable explanation that they may have for our own behavior (our disagreement with them).

Copycat Suicides?

The ultimate form of conformity was studied by Phillips (1974, 1978, 1982) and described succinctly in a book called Influence by Robert Cialdini (ch. 4, pp. 118-131). Highly publicized suicides are apparently correlated with a substantial increase in suicides (and other deaths) following the publicized case. This correlation is specific to the geographic region in which the case was publicized and specific to attributes of the suicide (e.g., age, gender, etc.), thus giving this phenomenon the name "Werther effect."

Determinants of Obedience in the Milgram Experiment

Finally, here is a summary of factors that help explain the surprisingly high rates of obedience in this classic experiment.

  • gradual increase of shock intensity with no cut-off point; if you administered x V, there is no reason to stop at x + 15V
  • no apparent "exit channel," no easy way to stop---in fact, the 450V mark on the shock generator is the closest thing to an endpoint ("If I get there, it will be over.")
  • diffusion of responsibility (righteous experimenter takes "all responsibility")
  • stubborn experimenter whose communication is odd and non-cooperative
  • subjects glide into a weird, confusing situation (cf. the Asch situation) in which no rational behavior is apparent
  • subjects experience tension and dissonance but find no way to reduce it
  • subjects' actions and cognitions are dissociated (they repeatedly protest and claim, "I can't go on," but they nevertheless do go on)
