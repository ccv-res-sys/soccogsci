Aggression and Violence

Phenomena

  • recall Milgram study: socially facilitated/dictated aggression
  • war, capital punishment socially institutionalized aggression (cf. Dead Man Walking, an excellent movie on that topic)
  • socially condoned acts of aggression (e.g., self-defense, saving someone else)
  • compare: a 3-year-old throwing toys at other kids; a nasty, snapping roommate; a 32-year-old wiping out his own family; a US president bombing another country; a father yelling at his child.

We label, interpret, and explain these aggressive acts very differently, taking into account many context factors and social/cultural norms. Identifying aggression is in part a social labeling process.

Affective aggression Instrumental aggression
= aggressive behavior caused by negative affect such as anger, hatred, frustration; this negative affect is in turn typically elicited by a situational stimulus. = aggressive behavior done for a particular purpose: organized crime, self-defense, war, terrorism.

Where Does Violence Come From?

A. Some of the many factors that can instigate acts of aggression:
  • biological basis: genetic factors influence temperament and physical strength; along with social shaping, they can lead up to antisocial and violent personality traits; from a 1997 study that reviewed dozens of twin studies: self-reported aggression has on average a 20-30% heritability, but when behavior is measured, heritability goes down to 0% (evidence for strong social influence on behavior? more so than on personality?)
  • learning of aggressive behavior repertoires (espec. in immediate family, neighborhood, and in the media), both operant conditioning and observational learning; not learning of empathy, moral norms, fear of punishment
  • abuse experiences and other humiliations
  • poverty, unemployment, discrimination---more generally, the lack of opportunities to walk along the "normal" social paths towards life goals
  • lack of self-sanctioning (e.g., because no norms were internalized from family; because of social isolation and therefore deindividuation; because of hopeless future outlook and hence indifference towards potential negative consequences of one's actions)
  • culture of violence (TV, movies, news, video games, guns, daily hostility, etc.), which makes aggression and violence to be a "normal" response.

    Huesmann's long-term study on girls: Higher rates of TV watching in childhood (especially between 6 and 8 years of age) perdicted higher rates of aggression,anti-social, and criminal behavior.

    From a 1995 study on violence and TV: perpetrators of violent acts go unpunished 73% of the time; 47% of all violent interactions show no harm to the victim, 58% depict no pain, long-term consequences (e.g., emotional, financial) are shown only 16% of the time; only 4% of programs containing violence emphasize non-violent conflict resolution.

B. The three main factors that maintain ongoing levels of violence:

  • reconstrual of own behavior as justified and acceptable
  • dehumanization of victim (a form of dissonance reduction after acting violently)
  • culture of violence (see above)

C. Functions of aggression: There is little evidence that aggression is itself a drive that needs to be fulfilled. Rather, aggression functions in helping fulfill other goals -- e.g., self-worth, power, and social equity.

What Can we do About Violence?

  • Punishment (threatened or actual)
  • An alternative to punishment that only slowly gains political force is Early Intervention: the attempt to assess and retrain "antisocial" children at the earliest possible age in order to prevent them from falling into the highly predictive path of increasing aggression. If you are interested to learn more, look at Zigler et al. (1992). Early childhood intervention: A promising preventative for juvenile delinquency. American Psychologist, 47, 997-1006. Also, check out the webpage of the Early Preventionists Network.
  • Catharsis (see Aronson)
  • Non-aggressive models (contagion of restraint)
  • Reconstrual (distraction, reinterpretation of anger or of provocation, anger management)
  • Self-sanctioning training (learning social skills, inhibition, individuation, standards, fear)
  • Awareness of roots (affect, reliable "triggers," culture, interpersonal style)
