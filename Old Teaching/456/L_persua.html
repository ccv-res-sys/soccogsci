Persuasion and Compliance

Distinction between social influence concepts

Conformity = a target's (conscious or unconscious) adjustment of behavior or belief in assimilation to a real or imagined group (e.g., imitation, following fashion trends, adopting cool and awesome new words into one's vocabulary)

Persuasion = an individual's intentional act of influencing a target's beliefs or attitudes (often using media such as writing, TV, film, etc.)

Compliance = a target's behavior in accordance with another's attempt to get the target to behave that way (e.g., via a request or manipulative tactics)

Obedience = a target's compliance with an authority who demands that the target perform the behavior

Later research also took time into account -- some persuasion tactics work briefly but do not last, whereas others do not work immediately but have long-term effects.

For example: Sleeper effect. A high-credibility (high expertise) communicator is more successful immediately after the persuasion, but a low-credibility (low expertise) communicator catches up and is as effective after a few weeks. (Explanation: people forget who the communicator was and only remember the message. The power of the communicator effect -- be it positive or negative -- lasts only for a while.)

Persuasion research in the 80s and 90s

The major assumption of newer research is that persuasive attempts can be directed along two routes:

  1. Central route: Transparent appeal to the conscious and deliberative mind of the target, typically involving arguments and information. Leads to systematic processing.

  2. Peripheral route. Non-transparent or concealed appeal to the unconscious mind of the target, typically involving emotions, desires, habits, associations. Leads to heuristic processing. By far the more studied route, both in experimental and applied psychology.
Two important factors that determine whether the target engages in one of the other processing mode:

  • Motivation:
    If the target person is motivated to be accurate and to learn as much as possible about the topic -- e.g., because the outcome is personally relevant -- then systematic processing is likely and a central-route persuasion will work most effectively.

  • Attention:
    If the target person is overloaded with other tasks, he or she cannot pay careful attention to the persuasive message, leading to heuristic processing. If not other distractions are present, however, systematic processing is possible.
The two routes of persuasion (and their associated processes) shed new light on the factors that the Yale model studied, such that some classic persuasive tactics work as long as the target engages in systemtic processing but do not work when the target "tunes out" into heuristic processing, and vice versa. For example, distraction leads to heuristic processing and increases the effect of emotional tone, status of messenger, etc. A forewarning, by contrast, triggers attention and motivation to use systematic processing and increases the effectiveness of rational or two-sided arguments.

Compliance

Recently, the social-psychological research on compliance has been summarized by Robert Cialdini in his book Influence. Cialdini describes six main weapons of influence that people use to influence others' behaviors -- from car salespeople to politicians and young children.

1. RECIPROCITY -- If the communicator gives a little something, the target feels obliged to give something back, which makes you vulnerable to the persuasive attempt.
Special example: Door-in-the-face-technique (communicator has a big request first, the target refuses, then communicator "concedes" by asking for a smaller favor, target has a harder time refusing a second time.)

2. SCARCITY -- rare stamps, rare cars, end-of-bin sale, offer ends at midnight...
This process works best if the scarcity does in fact suggests higher value ("going-out-of-business sales may not suggest that).

3. AUTHORITY (power, fame, expertise)

4. COMMITMENT -- If the communicator can get the target to commit to one thing first, then persuading him or her to do another thing that is consistent with the initial one is easier to achieve than without the initial one.
Foot-in-the-door technique: small request first, the target agrees; a bigger request, which to refuse would mean for the target to be inconsistent, is then easier to push through.
Low-balling:

Getting the target to commit to an attractive option first (e.g., a cheap sale price on a car), then making the option less attractive (e.g., price did not include floor mats, price had a calculation error).

5. LIKING/EGO-BOOST -- compliments, persuasion by liked and familiar targets

6. SOCIAL VALIDATION -- classic conformity to social norms (e.g., others vote for X, so I should too; 9 out of 10 people prefer Flakey cereal. . . )
