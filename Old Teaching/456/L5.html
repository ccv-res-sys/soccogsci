Persuasion

Persuasion = a communicator's intentional attempt to change a target's opinion, belief, attitude, or behavior. This attempt can be made along two routes:

  1. Central route. Transparent appeal to the conscious and deliberative mind of the target, typically involving arguments and information.

  2. Peripheral route. Non-transparent or even concealed appeal to the unconscious mind of the target, typically involving emotions, desires, habits, associations. By far the more studied route, both in experimental and applied psychology.

We discussed two traditions of persuasion research, the classic Yale model (1950s), which is also described in detail by Aronson, and the more recent research on compliance and attitude change, as summarized by Cialdini in his book Influence.

Not so obvious: Sleeper effect. A high-credibility (high expertise) communicator is more successful immediately after the persuasion, but a low-credibility (low expertise) communicator catches up and is as effective after a few weeks. (Explanation: people forget who the communicator was and only remember the message. The power of the communicator effect -- be it positive or negative -- lasts only for a while.

Weapons of influence

1. RECIPROCITY -- If the communicator gives a little something, the target feels obliged to give something back, which makes you vulnerable to the persuasive attempt.
Special example: Door-in-the-face-technique (communicator has a big request first, the target refuses, then communicator "concedes" by asking for a smaller favor, target has a harder time refusing a second time.)

2. SCARCITY -- rare stamps, rare cars, end-of-bin sale, offer ends at midnight...
This process works best if the scarcity does in fact suggests higher value ("going-out-of-business sales may not suggest that).

3. AUTHORITY (power, fame, expertise)

4. COMMITMENT -- If the communicator can get the target to commit to one thing first, then persuading him or her to do another thing that is consistent with the initial one is easier to achieve than without the initial one.
Foot-in-the-door technique: small request first, the target agrees; a bigger request, which to refuse would mean for the target to be inconsistent, is then easier to push through.
Low-balling:

Getting the target to commit to an attractive option first (e.g., a cheap sale price on a car), then making the option less attractive (e.g., price did not include floor mats, price had a calculation error).

5. LIKING/EGO-BOOST -- compliments, persuasion by liked and familiar targets

6. SOCIAL VALIDATION -- classic conformity to social norms (e.g., others vote for X, so I should too; 9 out of 10 people prefer Flakey cereal. . . )
