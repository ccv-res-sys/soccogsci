Aggression and Violence

Phenomena

  • recall Milgram study: socially facilitated/dictated aggression
  • war, capital punishment: socially institutionalized aggression (cf. Dead Man Walking, an excellent movie on that topic)
  • socially condoned acts of aggression (e.g., self-defense, saving someone else)
  • compare: a 3-year-old throwing toys at other kids; a nasty, snapping roommate; a 32-year-old wiping out his own family; a US president bombing another country; a father yelling at his child.

We label, interpret, and explain these aggressive acts very differently, taking into account many context factors and social/cultural norms. The complexity of labeling aggression is in part a reflection of the complexity of aggression itself.

Affective aggression Instrumental aggression
= aggressive behavior caused by negative affect such as anger, hatred, frustration; this negative affect is in turn typically elicited by a situational stimulus. = aggressive behavior done for a particular purpose: organized crime, self-defense, war, terrorism.

This model summarizes a good number of the factor that have been identified in the literature as determinants of aggression. Preventing aggression can start with preventing any combination of these factors because they increase the probablity of aggressive behavior. A powerful factor is omitted, however, in this model: The inhibition of aggressive behavior despite having an aggressive impulse. Recent research has looked at these inhibtory and "self-sanctioning" processes, which are powerful regulators of aggressive behavior (e.g., recent work by Albert Bandura; anger management techniques). These processes are so important because they provide a "buffer" against aggression. Almost everybody will get into situations that trigger an aggressive impulse (someone cutting you off, insulting you, etc.); the critical issue is whether one acts aggressively in this angry state.

Where Does Violence Come From?

A. Many factors can instigate acts of aggression:
  • Biological basis: Genetic factors influence temperament and physical strength, and along with social shaping, they can lead up to antisocial and violent personality traits. A 1997 study reviewed dozens of twin studies on the genetic basis of aggression and found that self-reported aggression has on average a 20-30% heritability, but when behavior is measured, heritability is near 0% (evidence for strong social influence on aggressive behavior as opposed to aggressive personality?)

  • Learning of aggressive behavior repertoires (espec. in immediate family, neighborhood, and in the media), both operant conditioning and observational learning. Equally important is the failure to learn empathy, moral self-sanctioning, guilt, shame, fear of punishment and other inhibitory mechanisms.

  • Experiences of abuse and other humiliations can build up intolerable levels of frustration, and the observation of others' behavior of abuse leads to observation learning.

  • Poverty, unemployment, discrimination---more generally, the lack of opportunities to walk along the "normal" social paths toward socially acceptable life goals.

  • Lack of self-sanctioning (e.g., because no norms were internalized from family; because of social isolation and therefore deindividuation; because of hopeless future outlook and hence indifference towards potential negative consequences of one's actions).

  • Culture of violence (TV, movies, news, video games, guns, daily hostility, etc.), which makes aggression and violence into a "normal" response that is often reinforced by the success (e.g., domination, status) it breeds.

    Huesmann's long-term study on girls: Higher rates of TV watching in childhood (especially between 6 and 8 years of age) predicted higher rates of aggression,anti-social, and criminal behavior.

    From a 1995 study on violence and TV: perpetrators of violent acts go unpunished 73% of the time; 47% of all violent interactions show no harm to the victim, 58% depict no pain, long-term consequences (e.g., emotional, financial) are shown only 16% of the time; only 4% of programs containing violence emphasize non-violent conflict resolution.

B. The three main factors that maintain ongoing levels of violence:

  • reconstrual of own behavior as justified and acceptable
  • dehumanization of victim (a form of dissonance reduction after acting violently)
  • culture of violence (see above)

C. Functions of aggression: There is little evidence that aggression is itself a drive that needs to be fulfilled. Rather, aggression functions in helping fulfill other goals -- e.g., self-worth, power, and social equity. Even if aggression had an innate component shared with animals, that type of aggression would not explain the specific human aggression and violence (serial killing, school shooting, domestic violence, etc.)

What Can we do About Violence?

  • Punishment (threatened punishment works if it is believable; actual punishment works only if it comes immediately after the aggressive act and is intensely aversive)
  • An alternative to punishment that is only slowly gaining political force is early intervention: the attempt to assess and retrain "antisocial" children at the earliest possible age in order to prevent them from falling into the highly predictive path of increasing aggression. If you are interested to learn more, look at Zigler et al. (1992). Early childhood intervention: A promising preventative for juvenile delinquency. American Psychologist, 47, 997-1006. Also, check out the webpage of the Early Preventionists Network.

  • Catharsis doesn't work (see Aronson). There may be some forms of mild catharsis that are succssful such as a physical work-out (would fall under anger management).

  • Non-aggressive models (contagion of restraint).

  • Reconstrual (distraction, reinterpretation of anger or of provocation, anger management). This technique is especially imnportant in relationships where misunderstandings about provocations can quickly escalate.

  • Self-sanctioning training (learning social skills, inhibition, individuation, standards, fear). This includes an awareness of roots (affect, reliable "triggers," culture) and appropriate choices to not expose oneself to triggers or aggression and if aggression is triggered, to lear techniques of behavior inhibition.
