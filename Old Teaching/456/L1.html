Attitudes and Behavior

Social Psychology

Social psychology studies two types of phenomena: (1) social behaviors (e.g., helping, aggression, romantic relationships) and (2) critical determinants of social behavior (e.g., attitudes, self-concept, dissonance reduction, presence of other people). These determinants can be roughly grouped into (1a) processes internal to the social agent and (2a) context factors. In class I showed a simple graph that illustrated the complex relations between context, internal processes, and social behaviors.

What is an attitude?

  • An attitude is directed to existing objects (e.g., issues, people, cars), it is evaluative (i.e., are affectively positive or negative), and enduring and dispositional.
  • Because attitudes are dispositions, social perceivers as well as psychologists must infer attitudes. One problem of inference is the unreliability of single indicators (e.g., self-report, GSR, facial expression). This is why scientists and laypeople alike should always use multiple indicators when inferring attitudes.
  • Because attitudes are enduring and dispositional, they must be stored somewhere, and the specific indicators of the attitude must be caused by something.
    The schema model of attitudes tries to describe that "something." An attitude is thought of as a schema--that is, a network of representations (similar to a memory trace, but more complex). A schema thus integrates all components of attitudes because it represents (and can cause) beliefs, emotions, physiological reactions, behavioral tendencies, etc. The network can be "activated" through any one aspect (e.g., seeing the attitude object) and in turn activated the other aspects (e.g., the emotion, beliefs about the object, a tendency to approach the object). The schema model is also compatible with several possible neurological instantiations of networks (e.g., molecular, neuronal, holistic) and thus makes it plausible that attitudes actually exist somewhere in the brain.

Origins of attitudes

  • Imitation (conscious adoption, unconscious assimilation; Newcomb, 1943).
  • Information (e.g., perception, rumor, reading newspapers).
  • Reward and punishment (operant conditioning). Experiment by Hildum & Brown (1956): Phone interviews in which experimenter says "good" or "mm-hmm" in response to expressed opinions. These opinions became more strongly held attitudes, as tested one week later.
  • By association with basic pleasant/unpleasant reactions (classical conditioning).Some of these basic reactions are probably unlearned (e.g., physiological stress, pain, certain odors and sounds).
  • Genetic influence Tesser (1993). Some attitudes (e.g., towards the death penalty, religion, sex, music) show heritability coefficients of around .50. More heritable attitudes are harder to change, and they are more quickly activateable. Clearly, attitudes don't "sit" on genes; genetic influences on attitudes must be mediated through complex interactions between abstract biological tendencies and socio-cultural shaping.

Function of attitudes (as summarized by Katz, 1960)

  • Knowledge function: Attitudes store knowledge about objects and domains, and because of the affective (evaluative) component of attitudes, this knowldge quickly activated
  • Need satisfaction: Because of their affective component, attitudes help avoid "bad things" and help approach "good things"
  • Ego defense: Negative attitudes towards objects or people result in blaming and derogation, which is one method of achieving self-regard (sometimes this phenomenon is called "scapegoating").
  • Social identity: You are in part what you believe in and what you like; similar attitudes between people make them bond, opposing attitudes make them clash.

The attitude-behavior link

Doubts about the validity of this link have existed since LaPiere's (1934) famous study. Sociologists criticized the attitude concept in the 50s, and Wicker's (1969) review, as summarized in the textbook, was skeptical of any predictive value from attitudes to behaviors. In response to these doubts, researchers studied the conditions under which attitudes do predict behavior.

  1. Attitude side

    • Measurement: Assessment of multiple indicators improves prediction.
    • Direct experience (either during formation or during repeated encounters with the attitude object) strengthens attitude.
    • Strength of attitude (extremity, confidence, accessibility) improves prediction.
    • Activation (e.g., through encountering the attitude object; through mirrors to increase self-awareness; through reminders of one's attitude): activated attitudes cause behavior more reliably. Further reading on awareness: Joko Beck, Everyday Zen.

  2. Behavior side

    • Measurement: Aggregating several behavior assessments (across multiple occasions, different situations) improves prediction.
    • Channel factors vs. counteracting forces. Channel factors improve prediction--interpersonal support (e.g., your friends help you not to drink when you drive), chains of behaviors (e.g., blood donation drive with sign-ups, maps, etc.). Counteracting forces decrease prediction--situational pressures (e.g., economic depression during LaPiere's study), peer pressure (e.g., attitudes toward drug use predict only behavior alone, not among peers).

  3. Link itself

    • Measurement: Temporal delay between attitude and behavior assessment decreases prediction.
    • Compatibility (attitudes and behaviors should be on similar level of specificity to increase prediction). For example, a study on birth control attitudes by Davidson & Jaccard (1979) showed that when the attitude question was abstract ("What is your attitude toward birth control?"), the correlation with actual use of birth control pills over a 2-year perdiod was minimal (r = .08). However, when the attitude question was as concrete as the behavior ("What is your attitude toward using birth control pills during the next 2 years?"), the correlation increased to r = .57.
