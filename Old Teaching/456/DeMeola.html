An Exploration of Cognitive Dissonance Among Friends

Copyright by Marissa DeMeola

October 28, 2002

Although people may not know it’s name, everyone at one time or another has felt the unpleasant psychological tension that comes with choosing between attitudes and behaviors that are contradictory. Most often this feeling stems from situations involving attitude formation and change, especially relevant to decision-making and problem solving. These unpleasant feelings are attributed cognitive dissonance, a concept that was introduced by Leon Festinger in 1957.

In general, people are most comfortable when their cognitions are in harmony with each other. Whether this concept stems from the nature of the human organism or whether it is learned during the process of socialization, people appear to prefer cognitions that fit together rather than those that do not. According to cognitive dissonance theory, two cognitions are dissonant if one cognition is opposite of another. A person who has dissonant cognitions can be considered in a state of psychological dissonance, which is the unpleasant psychological tension that is felt. This state of tension acts as a motivator for the person to ease the dissonance. Also, in the case of a discrepancy between attitudes and behavior, it is possible that the attitude will change to accommodate the behavior.

To confirm these predictions, Festinger and Carlsmith (1959) performed an experiment to which they had a group of people work on a boring task for an hour. Next, the experimenter asked the subjects to explain to the next person (a confederate) that the task was actually interesting. After agreeing to help, the subjects were each paid a sum of money, either $1 or $20. As this theory predicts, the subjects who were paid only $1 liked the experiment more than those who were paid $20. The people who received $20 did not experience dissonance because the large sum of money justified their behavior. However, the people who were paid only $1 had no such justification. Instead, telling someone else that the experiment was interesting contradicted his or her boredom during the experiment. To reduce their dissonance, these subjects altered their memory by claiming that they actually did enjoy the experiment (Malle, 1994).

People are constantly asking their friends for favors, and most often people comply with what is asked of them. However, in the past, I have been asked to do a favor for a friend that is tedious and time-consuming. I have noticed that although I do the favor, I seem to adjust my attitude to comply with the behavior because to do otherwise would go against my general idea of myself as a good person and a good friend. For example, I tell my friend that the favor is not a problem and that I don’t mind. In doing so, I adjust my belief and convince myself that the favor really is no problem and that I am a good friend. Based on these past experiences, and on the Festinger and Carlsmith experiment, I decided to conduct an experiment of my own, using four of my close friends as unknowing participants. This experiment would test two questions: one, whether the participants’ attitudes would change to match their behavior and two, if an attitude change is more or less likely to occur if there is less incentive to do the favor. By observing the behavior of my friends I hoped to witness an example of cognitive dissonance. I hypothesized that the friends who were asked to do a "tedious errand" for me and given an incentive to do so would be more likely to admit how tedious and annoying the errand was. Further, the friends who were not given the incentive would change their attitude about the errand and tell me that the task was not a problem in order to reduce their dissonance.

Method

The participants were undergraduate students at the University of Oregon, two men and two women, chosen because of their close friendship with me. I called each subject in the morning, telling him or her that I was not feeling well, and asked if they would run what I determined to be a "tedious errand" for me. The task was to get a book from the library on campus, which I said I needed to write a paper but was "too sick" to get myself. I also requested that they bring the book to my house. This errand was determined to be tedious due to the hassle of having to get the book from the library and also because of the time spent in getting the book and bringing it to my house. To measure whether subjects’ attitudes would change to avoid dissonance I created two groups: participants who received an incentive, or gift (the "low dissonance" condition) and participants who did not receive an incentive (the "high dissonance" condition). Two of the subjects (one male and one female) were chosen at random to receive the incentive. In this case, the incentive was a gift of a mixed CD made especially for them and a picture of the two of us. After asking them to get the book and bring it to me, I told these participants that I had made them a CD and that I had a picture to give them, while the other two subjects were only asked to do the tedious errand. Each participant agreed to do what was asked of him or her. Data were collected by interviewing the participant through what was disguised as normal conversation where I observed the person’s behavior as well as what they thought about the task they did for me.

Results

The results of this experiment support my hypothesis that the friends who did not receive an incentive but did the tedious errand would change their attitude to match their behavior. In general, these friends told me that they were happy to do the favor for me and it was not out of their way. My female friend who did not receive the incentive and was hypothesized to have high dissonance said, "It was no problem, and I’m glad to help out". Further, the male participant who did not receive an incentive said, "It was no problem because I had to go to the library anyway. It’s fine". Further supporting my hypothesis, the friends who received the incentive generally expressed an attitude that they did not enjoy doing the favor but were still happy to help out. The female subject who received the incentive said, "This entailed a lot more than you said at first, but it was okay because you don’t usually ask me to do things like this", and the male participant said, "This stressed me out because I had other things to do today, but I knew you needed the favor". Both participants in the low dissonance category expressed more negative feelings about the errand than the participants in the high dissonance category.

Discussion

This study was very interesting to me. If I had not studied cognitive dissonance or read about some of the more technical and controlled experiments that help to support this theory, my common sense would have told me that the results would have been opposite of how they turned out. For instance, one might expect that those without gifts would have more negative feedback about the errand because they were not compensated for their time, while those who got a gift would be expected to say how much they did not mind helping out because they received something for their efforts. However, because of the dissonance felt by the participants who did not receive a gift, they were more inclined to believe that the favor was not a problem.

Also interesting to me was that, after I revealed the real reason for the errand (this experiment), all four of my friends confessed to how much they did not enjoy what I asked them to do. Nevertheless, it is my belief that this information was only provided to me because in my telling them the truth about the task I was, in a way, letting them off the hook. This could have acted to ease their dissonance. I also ended up giving them all a mixed CD as well as a picture for being unknown participants in my experiment and for being good sports about it.

This experiment was not very controlled and had many limitations. First, the particular attitudes, as well as the schedule, of each friend for that particular day could possibly have altered the results. Also, the male friend who did not receive the incentive told me that the task was no problem because he had to go to the library anyway. Therefore, he may not have found the errand as tedious as the other participants did. Another limitation on this experiment is that the gift I offered may have meant more to the female participant than the male participant and vice versa, which also could have altered the results.

Cognitive dissonance theory is intriguing to me, because although everyone has been in this state before and felt the psychological tension it causes, it is not often that one consciously thinks about it. It was very interesting to see that my friends who did not receive an incentive fit the profile of a person trying to ease their dissonance. If my friends did in fact act according to the theory of cognitive dissonance, the subjects who received nothing had much less external justification and more dissonance. Therefore, they reduced their dissonance by changing their attitude toward the task. In this case, they believed that the errand was not so out of their way or that they really did not mind. This experiment is an example of how dissonance occurs in everyday life and in everyday situations, between experiences with strangers and with friends.
