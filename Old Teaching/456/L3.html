Social Perception

Social perception is the study of people making sense of other people and themselves. One can distinguish between two traditions -- one called "person perception" (which recognized that people see each other as agents who perform intentional actions with motives) and "social cognition" (which portrayed the social perceiver more like a computer that calculates, with many mistakes, the behaviors of others). Social cognition has been the dominant tradition in the last 20 years, but with the rise of developmental research on children's "theory of mind" the tide may turn a bit.

Attention to Behavioral Events

One issue in exploring person perception is what kinds of behaviors people pay attention to and try to explain. To answer this question we introduce a classification of behavioral events that results from a distinction into observable and unobservable behavioral events with a distinction into intentional and unintentional behavioral events:

IntentionalUnintentional
ObservableActionsMere Behaviors
UnobservableIntentional ThoughtsExperiences

Now we can ask which of these behaviors people attend to and explain. We need to distinguish between the "actor" role (people paying attention to their own behaviors) and the "observer" role (people paying attention to others). As actors, people pay most attention to their own experiences (which are unobservable and unintentional); as observers, they pay most attention to other people's actions (which are observable and intentional). The same asymmetry holds for the kinds of behavioral events actors and observers explain (see readings for next week).

History of attribution research

Fritz Heider

In his famous 1958 book, The psychology of interpersonal relations, Heider sketched out what he termed people's "naive theory of action"---the conceptual framework by which people interpret, explain, and predict others' behavior (nowadays, there are many labels for roughly the same: folk psychology, theory of mind, folk theory of behavior). In this framework, intentional concepts (e.g., beliefs, desires, trying, purpose) played a central role; but Heider also sometimes talked about the distinction between person and situation causes. The tension between intentional concepts on the one hand and the person-situation distinction on the other was never resolved; Heider did not clarify their relationship, and subsequent theories simply dropped intentional concepts and focused on the simpler person-situation distinction.

Jones and Davis (1965) analyzed how people infer dispositions from behavior, and they saw judgments of intention as prerequisites for judgments of dispositions (the inference chain was: action -> intention -> trait). But the focused entirely on the dispositional inferences and left important questions unanswered: What do people infer when the behavior is unintentional? And how do people relate intentional behavior to motives/reasons (such as beliefs and desires), as Heider suggested? Finally, how are these motives or reasons related to traits? (Some of these questions will be addressed in next week's readings.)

Theory of mind research

  • Why "theory"? -- Children may be born with a unique sensitivity to human action, being able to see structure within the stream of movement (see Baird & Baldwin's article for Thursday). As they "practice" their interpretations of human behavior, they acquire generalizations and concepts about the mind and learn to detect indicators of beliefs, desires, intentions, and intentional action.

  • First model: desires -> action. Probably the first mental concept children acquire is that of desire, which is very much action-bound. By 12 months infants seem to recognize the goal or target of someone's grasping movement (Woodward, 1998) and by 18 months they can perform an action after observing an adult attempt to perform that action (Meltzoff, 1998). This last finding is significant because children apparently infer from the adult's attempt what the full-fledged action should look like.

  • Second model: beliefs + desires -> action. Around the age of 4 children understand that beliefs subjectively represent reality and that different people can have different beliefs (and act on those beliefs, even if they are false). I described an experiment (by Wimmer & Perner, 1983) that tests for children's ability to ascribe false beliefs to other people. This ability also coincides with their capacity to take other people's (visual or psychological) perspective -- something that remains difficult even for adults (as we all know when we are in an argument with someone else).
