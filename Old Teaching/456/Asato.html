The Effect of Self-Image Maintenance on Prejudice

Copyright by Yugo Asato

November 13th, 2002

Hate crimes are among the most serious problems in the United States. Hate crimes against victims’ racial or ethnic origin, religious preference, and sexual orientation are most notorious, but the fact is that hate crimes can be aimed at anybody based on any factor. Stereotyping and prejudice are the main causes of hate crimes and are deep-rooted and hard to modify. It appears that stereotyping and prejudice are not merely a product of accumulated historical issues but also a product of psychological mechanisms that reinforce stereotyping and prejudice. In the article, "Prejudice as Self-Image Maintenance: Affirming the Self Through Derogating Others," Fein and Spencer (1997) discuss how self-image maintenance processes underlie stereotyping and prejudice.

Three studies were carried out to examine how self-image maintenance processes affect stereotyping and prejudice. In study 1, the authors hypothesized that a self-affirmation procedure should decrease the probability that participants evaluate a stereotyped group negatively. The self-affirmation procedure was to bolster self-worth, and, in this study, subjects were asked what value was important to them and were asked to explain why. Only half of the participants completed this procedure. Then the participants evaluated a target woman’s job qualification and overall personality based on the same job interview video excerpts, a job application including a photograph of the target woman, and other relevant information. However, minor pieces of information about the job candidate suggested that she was either Jewish or Italian American. For the participants, who were going to the University of Michigan, Jewish people were very likely to be considered as a stereotyped group.

The results of study 1 confirmed the hypothesis that individuals will be less likely to derogate members of stereotyped groups when self-affirmed. Self-affirmed participants evaluated the target almost equally regardless of the perceived target’s race. On the other hand, those participants who were not self-affirmed rated the target’s job qualification significantly more negatively than the self-affirmed participants if the target was seen as Jewish. The authors further analyzed the results of study 1 and suggested that non self-affirmed subjects might derogate the stereotyped target to affirm their self-image.

It is very interesting to see how the self-affirmation procedure that was not relevant to the evaluation of the target actually affected the participants and decreased the likelihood of negative evaluation. However, it is conceivable that it was not the self-affirmation procedure but the thinking process (thinking about anything in general) that influenced the participants to be more objective or open-minded. Consequently, participants might have rated the target equally regardless of the target’s perceived race. In future research, various types of thinking should be tested to determine whether only self-affirmation thinking decreases the likelihood of negative evaluation. In addition, this study should be replicated on other racial or cultural groups in order to examine whether the effect of self-affirmation might increase or decrease depending on the rigidity of stereotyped images that people possess for the those groups.

The objective of study 2 was to investigate whether the effect of stereotyping would be increased if a self-image threat were introduced to individuals. All the participants had to complete a bogus intelligence task. However, only half of the participants were given disappointing feedback, and the other half of the participants in the neutral condition were told that task was bogus. Thus, they did not need to try so hard to complete it. Then, the participants read a story about a target person named Greg. The story implied that he was either homosexual or heterosexual. At the end, the participants were asked to rate Greg’s personality on 10 dimensions, which consisted of three stereotype-irrelevant traits such as intelligence and seven stereotype-relevant traits such as femininity, sensitivity, passivity, etc.

The results of study 2 revealed that participants evaluated the target person portrayed as homosexual in a stereotyping way if they had previously received the self-image threatening negative feedback. This effect was not observed in participants who were in the neutral condition. Also, the manipulation of the feedback did not have a significant impact when the target was seen as heterosexual. This indicated that individuals exhibit stereotyping and prejudice specifically toward members of stereotyped groups when their self-image is threatened. Related to the results of study 1, the results of study 2 also suggested that people might be motivated to maintain their self-image, and stereotyping and prejudice can be a vehicle to do so.

One significant question concerning study 2 is to what extent individuals increase their prejudiced and stereotyping behaviors depending on their levels of self-esteem. On one hand, those with lower self-esteem might have many domains in which they feel incompetent. This would conceivably result in increased prejudice and stereotyping in order to improve their self-worth. On the other hand, people with high self-esteem might have many domains in which their high self-worth could be lowered or threatened, so they might be more prejudiced and use stereotypes to restore their self-image. In the future, researchers should examine the correlation between levels of self-esteem and probabilities of prejudiced and stereotyping behaviors. In addition, I have a little doubt as to the validity of the participants’ personality rating for the target person because they all might have different stereotype images for homosexual people. For example, the authors regarded intelligence as a stereotype-irrelevant trait, but certain people actually think that intelligence is one of the stereotyped characteristics for homosexual people. Then, this variance in stereotype images for homosexual people could have some detrimental impact on the accuracy of the study 2’s results.

The purpose of study 3 was to determine whether stereotyping and prejudice are really capable of restoring a threatened self-image. In study 3, all the participants had to take a bogus intelligence test and were given either positive or negative feedback. Then, they filled out a questionnaire that was designed to measure self-esteem. Finally, as in study 1, the participants were asked to rate the job qualification and overall personality of a target woman portrayed as either Jewish or Italian.

As expected, the participants who were given the positive feedback rated their self-esteem higher than those who had received the negative feedback. Those participants who were in the negative feedback condition evaluated the stereotyped target woman more negatively than those who had received the positive feedback. Furthermore, their self-esteem that was lowered by the negative feedback was restored by the negative evaluation of the target. Therefore, these results confirmed that stereotyping and prejudice can indeed restore a threatened self-imaged. Also, this finding has another significant implication, namely, that stereotyping and prejudice are not strongly socially unacceptable. If they were socially taboo, they would not restore individuals’ self-image but would further deteriorate their self-image.

Across the combined results of study 1, 2 and 3, I can observe how stereotyping and prejudice can become a vicious cycle. Because individuals are motivated to maintain their self-image, they need to restore their self-image when it is threatened. Stereotyping and prejudice, which are readily available in so many social situations, are then utilized to restore a threatened self-image. When a self-image is successfully restored, people can once again feel better about themselves. Consequently, this process has a reinforcing value on them. Furthermore, when those who are prejudiced or stereotyped by people in the self-image maintenance processes feel that their self-image is derogated, they might, in turn, be prejudiced against other groups or stereotype them.

In addition to the self-image maintenance processes, there is also confirmation bias. The confirmation bias refers to "a preference for information that is consistent with a hypothesis (or belief) rather than information which opposes it" (Plous, 1993, p. 233). For example, a person may have a stereotyped belief that all Asian people are hardworking. When this person meets an idle Asian man, he or she is motivated to see the man as an exception and ignore the fact that there are Asians who are not hardworking because it disconfirms his or her belief. This exemplifies how confirmation bias also facilitates prejudice and stereotype as self-image maintenance processes do.

This study reveals some significant aspects of stereotyping and prejudice in relation to the self-concept and perception of others. Moreover, the study successfully illustrates how self-image maintenance processes facilitate prejudice and stereotyping and internalize them in people. The most important message from the study is that we should be aware of the existence of these unconscious processes because it will be impossible to modify prejudiced behaviors unless we are aware of the processes. Being aware of these unconscious processes would be a first step for individuals to prevent prejudice and stereotyping.
