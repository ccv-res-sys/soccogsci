Folk Theory of Mind and Behavior I

Over two sessions we will discuss a relatively new perspective on social perception and interaction -- that of examining the concepts and implicit theories that people have about the human mind and behavior.

Interpersonal behavior among humans requires that people not only plan and perform their own behavior but also that they make sense of the other person's planning and performing of behavior. From the simple case of recognizing and greeting another person (which normally requires the perception that the other has recognized one too and is attentive enough and willing to reciprocate the greeting) to the complex cases of seeing through a scam, coordinating joint activities, or beginning a new relationship -- people's attempts to understand each other's emotions, beliefs, and goals is crucial to successful interpersonal interactions.

In philosophy, scholars have explored these topics for a while (under the label of "folk psychology" and in "philosophy of action") and have contributed to an understanding of the concepts by which people make sense of intentional human action. You will therefore see quite a few references to philosophical research in the articles for these two sessions. Even more active research on people theory of mind has come from developmental psychology, which explores how children learn to detect human intentions, begin to explain observable behavior with reference to mental states, and so on.

The papers for the first session give an overview of the topics and issues in this new field and describe in some more detail that part of the "theory of mind and behavior" that concerns intentional action and its explanation via beliefs and desires. Because this is one of my major research domains, you are getting a good dose of Malle papers, but that fact should not discourage you at all from being critical and skeptical.

In Malle (1997), I summarize a number of studies and topics, and this is really an introductory paper that builds on several other papers and studies. One of the papers it builds on, Malle and Knobe (1997), describes in detail how we identified the components of people's concept of intentionality. Even though many researchers had speculated about this concept, none bothered to collect any data on how people really conceptualize intentionality. This is a first set of data that tries to settle the issue. Many other questions remain -- are there cultural or gender differences in the use of this concept? Does our legal system use the same concept that people do? And how do children acquire each component of this concept?

The latter question is tackled by Henry Wellman (1991), who was here in Eugene a month ago for our conference on Intentionality. In his chapter, he reviews evidence (from his and other researchers' lab) on the steps that infants and children go through in acquiring concepts such as desire, belief, and ultimately intentionality.

In the spirit of evolutionary thinking, we might discuss what functions these concepts serve, why the intentionality concept has exactly those 5 components and not others, whether children are born with a "prewired" theory of mind and if not, how they acquire that complex theory. We might also address implications for the legal realm -- e.g., how can we decide whether someone did something intentionally? How does intentionality relate to responsibility?
