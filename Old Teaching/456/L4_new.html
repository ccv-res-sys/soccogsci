Personality: A Primer

History of Personality Research

  • Freud's "topological theory": Personality is the dynamic interplay between the id, the super-ego, and the ego. The ego is the regulating force that tries to maintain balance between the drives and needs of the id and the constraints and restrictions imposed by the super-ego. Failures to regulate can lead to psychopathology.

  • Behaviorism: No concern for traits (or any other unobservable phenomena). All behavior was to be explained by the reinforced habit patterns that the organism developed in the context of its environment.

  • Early trait theories: Gordon Allport argued that traits really exist inside the person, organizing responses to situational stimuli such that similar behavior tends to occur in response to certain classes of stimuli (e.g., presence of other people -> discomfort). Many of these traits were described as motivational in nature -- organizing action towards some goal. Little to no empirical research was done on Allport's trait theory.

  • Cognitive theories (Bandura, Mischel): These scholars postulated cognitive (mental) states as mediating between situational stimuli and behavior but did not believe in general, abstract traits. (They thus stand somewhat in the middle between behaviorism and trait theories.) The cognitive states that they focused on are expectations (beliefs).

  • Modern trait theories: On the basis of analyses of the lexicon (of the English and other languages), researchers found that personality terms can be organized along 5 broad dimensions. The names for these dimensions, which label one pole, and their opposite poles (in parentheses) are: Extraversion (introversion), Neuroticism (emotional stability), Agreeableness (disagreeableness), Conscientiousness (?), Openness to experience (?). Mnemonic (if you rearrange the order): O C E A N.

    Other personality dimensions that are not covered by the "Big 5" include self-consciousness, alexithymia, self-monitoring.

Genetics of Personality

I described how the "heritability coefficient" is derived and how it should be interpreted. Under the assumption that dizygotic twins (DZ) have on average half the amount of shared genes as monozygotic twins (MZ) have, and under the assumption that pairs of DZ and pairs of MZ both have "equal environments," we can claim that the correlation between MZ pairs (on some personality variable) is due to G (100% gene similarity) + E (some amount of similar environment), whereas the correlation between DZ pairs (on the same personality variable) is due to G/2 (50% gene similarity) + E (some amount of similar environment). The difference between the two correlations then allows us to solve for G:

MZ: r = .42, DZ: r = .22
.42 - .22 = (G + E) - (G/2 + E)
G = .40 (or 40%)

(In general, the difference between MZ and DZ correlations, if multiplied by two, gives you the heritability coefficient -- sometimes written as H or h2.)

What does a heritability coefficient of 40% mean? It means that 40% of the individual differences among people are due to their differences in genes, leaving about 60% to differences in their environments. However, subsequent studies corrected for some problematic assumptions and worked with twins reared together vs. apart, currently proposing a heritability of personality around 29% (compared to about 50% for schizophrenia).

Two Debates in Personality Psychology

I clarified the difference between the person-situation debate (which centers on the question to what degree any given behavior is caused by the agent's personality or by the situation) and the nature-nurture debate (which centers on the question to what degree personality is caused by genes or the environment).
