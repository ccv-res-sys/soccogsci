Week 1: What is Cognitive Science?

·      “Cognitive science is the interdisciplinary study of mind and the nature of intelligence.” (Indiana U website)

·      “The interdisciplinary study of minds, brains and behaviour.” (U College Dublin)

·      “The interdisciplinary study of cognitive processes in human and/or artificial systems” (Ping Li, U Richmond)

·      “Cognitive Science is the study of the mind from an interdisciplinary perspective.” (Brown U, Intro course)

·      “a common research objective: to discover the representational and computational capacities of the mind and their structural and functional representation in the brain” (Sloan Foundation Sate of the Art Report, 1978)

Disciplines

Philosophy, Linguistics, Psychology, Computer Science (AI), Neuroscience, Anthropology (Evolution)

Historical Context: behaviorism in psychology as a response to failed introspectionism; skepticism about Freud’s psychoanalysis; positivistic philosophy (operationalism); enormous success of physics (relativity theory, quantum physics, and the ensuing technological innovations); also success of chemistry, biology (evolution). Matter and life seemed on route to being understood; the puzzle of the mind had yet to be solved.  Side-effects of war: advances in the construction of number-crunching machines (e.g., for decoding) and “smart” weapons (e.g., forward-looking anti-aircraft guns).

Milestones

1944-1948:  Meetings organized by mathematicians von Neumann and Wiener, leading to cybernetics as a first model of planful human behavior (Wiener, 1948): an integrative theory of control and (internal) communication in machines, animals, and the brain.

1948: Hixon conference at CalTech on how the nervous system controls behavior.  Talks on how the brain might work like an electronic computer (vonNeumann) or a logic machine (McCulloch), how complex information processing must find its way back into psychology devastated by behaviorism and how hierarchical structures are far better at explaining language and planning than serial S-R models (Lashley).

Alan Turing, who already showed in 1936 that a simple binary machine could perform an infinite number of complex calculations and programs, pushed the mind=computer equation: In 1950 he published Computing Machinery and Intelligence in the philosophical journal Mind, arguing that computers would in time be programmed to acquire abilities rivaling human intelligence. http://www.abelard.org/turpap/turpap.htm

         As part of his argument Turing put forward the idea of an 'imitation game', in which a human being and a computer would be interrogated under conditions where the interrogator would not know which was which, the communication being entirely by textual messages. Turing argued that if the interrogator could not distinguish them by questioning, then it would be unreasonable not to call the computer intelligent.

            Turing's 'imitation game' is now usually called 'the Turing test' for intelligence.

            Turing's had his own doubts about AI — doubts centered on the serious problem of where to draw a line between thinking and living.

            A perhaps more central point in Turing’s argument was that that the operations of the brain must be computable.  That, in turn, would make machine intelligence possible.  Turing also put forward constructive arguments for how machine intelligence should be achieved.

            Wider cultural effects: e.g., HAL in A. C. Clarke’s 2001

For more information, see http://www.turing.org.uk/turing/scrapbook/ace.html, http://plato.stanford.edu/entries/turing-test/

In 1956, Newell and Simon completed the first-ever proof on a computer (machine), and in the same year, they joined two other scholars (McCarthy and Minsky) in a groundbreaking meeting on artificial intelligence—or the machine programs that could perform intelligent behaviors. (In 1972, Newell and Simon published 15 years of research and their theory of the brain as a general problem solver in Human Problem Solving.)

Around the same time, psychologists finally escaped the claws of behaviorism and began to study mental processes, such as perception, memory, and thinking, as a form of information processing (Miller, Bruner, Mandler).

In 1958, John von Neumann (also founder of subjective probability theory), published his book on The Computer and the Brain, an analysis of some common concepts in mind and machines (e.g., memory).   

In the same decade, neuroscientists succeeded in isolating neurons (in animals) that had highly specific functions; anthropologists began to study linguistic and cognitive regularities in different cultures (e.g., categorization), which became the field of  cognitive anthropology (first textbook in 1969); linguists plunged into the massive endeavor of deciphering the implicit cognitive transformations that the human mind undergoes when forming syntactic language (pioneered by Chomsky); and philosophers posed hard questions about the mind and its relation to the brain and other machines.

Signs of Establishment

1960 "Plans and the Structure of Behavior," by G. A. Miller, E. Galanter, K. Pribram.  Based on cybernetics, control theory, recursive feedback processes, but applied to the higher-order functions of planning and intentional action.  Highly influential book.

1960 Harvard Center for Cognitive Studies (Bruner and Miller).

Within psychology, experimental psychology became “cognitive psychology” and textbooks were published under that new name.

Large boost for research activity: $20 million  of funding from the Sloan Foundation in the mid-70s.

1977 Cognitive Science journal was founded and the society of the same name.

 

Core Insights

The mind learns about reality via subjective, constructive representations.

Language is grounded in transformational grammar, which allows it to be “generative” (a finite number of elements can be combined into an infinite number of statements) and learned by children without having been exposed to each and every possible statement.

Distinction between controlled and automatic processes; the mind must rely on both to function optimally.

Limitations of AI have become apparent (e.g., frame problem).

More recently, social factors in the evolution of mind are recognized, as well as the broader relationship between adaptations such as intelligence, social cognition, art, religion, and mathematical ability.

Quite recently, the importance of imagination, simulation, planning, affect, and the link between perception and action have been analyzed.

Limitations

  • Difficulty of any interdisciplinary research (time, multiple commitments and identities, no shared language, and key terms, even if they appear shared, may differ in meaning)
  • no agreed-upon research paradigm--no consensual set of assumptions or methods

o      candidates: rational argument, empirical science, naturalism; but far too broad—hold for every science.

  • One reason for the lack of unity: controversies over quite fundamental issues:

o      Cognition is representation (vs. embodied)

o      Cognition is computation (rules) vs. distributed processes (no rules)

o      Consciousness (vs. unconscious processes)

o      Free will (vs. determinism, illusory choice)

o      Modularity, innateness, plasticity, neural localization, unique human abilities, role of emotion, social cognition, self-awareness

 

Gardner’s Five Features of Cognitive Science

1. Representations

 


             

 

What is the “language of thought” (Fodor)? Rules, symbols, schemas, images? Or is there nothing but nerve firings?

2. Computers

“demonstrations of "thinking" in the computer in the 1950s” (Gardner, p. 40).  Really?

Fundamentally, computers and minds process information. Thus, computers can model (simulate) some of the mind’s information processing.  But the computer metaphor may very much limit the scope of what is studied as “mind.” It certainly took a while until emotions were integrated into cognitive science.  Skepticism about consciousness, qualia, free will, and the like may in part stem from the conviction that the mind must be like a computer (machine) and so cannot have those unique features.

            The role of computers has been diminishing in the past 15 years of cognitive science, while the neurosciences have integrated some computational ideas more comfortably.

3. Little Concern for affect, context, culture, history
Has begun to change in the past 10 years.
4. Interdisciplinary commitment

It’s what keeps the endeavor together, despite lack of a unifying paradigm.  (Gardner’s possible scenario of a fusion is not an issue at all these days.  Very few actual academic departments of Cognitive Science exist (one is at UCDS).
