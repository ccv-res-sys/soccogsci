Week 6 (May 1): Interpersonal Moral Sentiments

The Blame Family

  • Family members: Anger, blame, resentment, indignation, contempt.
    In this order, they range from the hot and largely automatic anger to the cooler contempt that may be more under intentional control.

  • Many of these negative moral sentiments arise automatically and require regulation of their public expression.

  • Rozin et al. would argue that contempt is more a reaction to a community norm violation and anger more a reaction to an autonomy norm violation. However, the two sentiments differ in their time course, so a comparison that only looks at hindsight judgments may be difficult. Moreover, anger and blame seem to occur even in cases of community norm violations.

  • Some or all these sentiments can be followed by expressed punishment -- verbal, social, or physical.

  • The sentiments are always backward-looking (responding to another person's violation) and sometimes also forward-looking (attempting to change future behavior). But these forward-looking considerations can also server as rationalizations for punishment or retaliatory aggression.

  • There are clear differences in intensity and differentiation of moral sentiments between (a) the target of the morally violating act (e.g., the victim) and (b) the observer.
The Praise Family

  • The family members: Appreciation, praise, gratitude, respect, admiration.
    The order reflects a time course, with appreciation being immediate and perhaps automatic and respect and admiration taking longer to build up, perhaps being directed at character and thus requiring more than one act.

  • Whereas the blame family has differentiated private emotions such as anger, hatred, or disgust at their core, the praise family does not necessarily come with a characteristic "appreciative" emotion. The feelings of pleasure that one may have when being the recipient or observer of a morally positive act may be rather undifferentiated. Praise is typically a social act of acknowledging a moral behavior and may not come with a defining emotion. Gratitude may be the closest to a private emotion, but it is complex, perhaps even involving guilt, because one is in debt of moral action (cf. feeling indebted to someone).

  • Positive moral sentiments may be less frequently triggered (because of general positive-negative asymmetries in habituation, need for response, social expectations, etc.), and they may be less automatic. That is, regulation may more often be directed towards the detection of praiseworthy acts rather than their expression (although even the appropriate contexts of praise expressions and rewards are socially regulated).

  • Positive moral sentiments are often backward-looking (responding to a praiseworthy act). In addition, they can be used for manipulative or teaching purposes, training/conditioning someone to commit more of the positive acts in the future.

  • Target and observer may have very similar responses to a moral act; the intensity difference appears smaller than that between target and observer of a violating moral act.

  • Puzzle: Why does excessive praise or gratitude for an actor create guilt in the actor? Perhaps the actor has a very different perspective on the action (it seemed natural, simply the right thing to do) than does the praiser (it seems extraordinary, perhaps with enormous positive consequences). Perhaps feelings of equity are violated if one perceives to receive large amounts of benefits for a small action. Perhaps modestly teaches people to not feel too strongly the warm glow of receiving praise.
