Paper Topics

  1. Attributions of responsibility in parent/child relationships. How does one learn how to attribute responsibility? Focus on this learning process and how this emerges in the relationship. One area of significance is the misattribution of relationships, which can lead to pathologies in adolescent life.

  2. Death penalty, perhaps focusing on the role of retribution, the law of retaliation, or models of reconciliation/forgiveness/empathy.

  3. Judgement of a child's intentionality. Relating to (a) the idea of "adult time" for "adult crime." Is there a way to view childrens' intentionality in crime from a perspective that goes beyond the severity of that crime? (b) parental misattribution of intentionality: the case of an undergrad who is a father asking why his 13-month-old "insists" on waking him up. Is there a perception that younger children understand less severe outcomes more completely than they understand more severe outcomes of misbehaving?

  4. Discrepancies between self and other in the blaming process. (a) Related to the sharing of attributions/sharing of resources, and how forgiveness/blame plays into the relationship with the "close other." (b) What is the function of blame? the dysfunction of blame? How does it influence the social order? When does a community "over-blame?"

  5. Role of moral sentiments within motivation

  6. Moral dimension of emotions (a) Morality of coping and the values implied in having/expressing emotions. (b) What distinguishes moral from non-moral sentiments?

  7. Either (a) Does moral reasoning depend on counterfactual thinking? Is there a developmental link to be made here? Or (b) Can embodied realism's approach to understanding causation (Mark Johnson) contribute to understanding how blame is assigned?

Is he (a) trying to get a date with the owner of the shop? (b) trying to ease his guilt at making a way-above-average (for Eugene) amount of money working for Hyundai? (c) Can Smith's analytical triad help in understanding Karl's behavior? our moral judgement of Karl's behavior?

Smith's model

  • The impartial spectator: imagine an action and try to judge the motives of the agent and receiver. The same apparatus is involved in judging both.

  • Moral sentiments are guided by what an impartial spectator would theorize if s/he were present.

  • Smith's theory requires an invariant moral receiver/observer with a defined set of moral values. He assumes a certain universality in emotion (i.e., the "reasonable man" approach). However, he rejects the concept that women can be normative moral obervers and lumps them in with "feeble-minded men."

  • Smith may underestimate the intensity of emotion involved in moral judgement, thus mischaracterizing moral sentiments. For Smith, cognitive processes of causation and propriety are placed at the fore.

  • Smith's approach requires a stepping-back from direct involvement, a psychological distance. This distance may be conceived as a two-dimensional surface as a function of two variables: time from action/reception and relational identification (or empathy, where this is understood as the identification of emotional and/or cognitive states, as opposed to mere sympathy).

  • Smith's view is egocentric. Distance along the relational identification axis can be modulated by self. The more empathic (that is, other-identified) one is, the less impartial and thus, according to Smith, the less qualified to make moral judgements. From this, it may be said that Smith's theory bears more weight as a prescriptive than a descriptive work.
