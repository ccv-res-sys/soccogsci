Week 3 (April 10): Responsibility

The class began with the observation that the majority of models we have read stress a rational approach to determining responsibility and assigning blame that gives inadequate attention to the role of emotions (particularly negative) and cultural/individual differences. In response, we have been constructing a model of "moral responses" to transgressions that addresses these deficits. Our current model involves at least 5 distinct phases that are outlined below.

Phase 1: Initial Reaction

1.1 Perceive a violation of expectation/norm.

a. Expectations and norms will vary from culture to culture and individual to individual.

1.2 Arousal/Vigilance state is activated.

1.3 Event categorization, centers on the question: "What happened?"

a. In an ambiguous situation, the arousal state might look like confusion or fear and will be accompanied by a wondering about what happened.

b. In an unambiguous situation, the person will quickly assume a causal agent. We may also have a default reaction to the event type (feeling sad if a family heirloom is broken).

1.4 Assumption of a casual agent (i.e. "Someone did this")

a. If the causal agent is familiar to us, we may activate a default response to that person (e.g., based on past interactions, liking or disliking).

1.5 Rudimentary causal search, centers on the questions: "Who did it? What was his/her intention?"

a. Individuals will vary in the depth and deliberateness of this initial search.

Phase 2: Emotion Differentiation

2.1 Emotion Differentiation: Arousal/vigilance plus information gathered in Phase 1 converge onto a specific emotional state such as anger, sadness, pity, or fear.

a. Culture may help determine how we pair specific events with specific emotions

b. There may be individual differences in a propensity to respond with particular emotions.

Phase 3: Blame 1

3.1 Accusation: Perceiver assigns preliminary responsibility for expectation/norm violation to identified agent. May or may not act on blame at this point. (Meg has termed this the "smack or stew" phase)

The process ends for some people (they stew).

Phase 4: Considerations of Responsibility

[This is generally where the published blame models begin.]

4.1 In an effort to re-assess the person's responsibility (blameworthiness), the perceiver considers a variety of factors such as: controllability, mitigating factors (the person's intentions, capacities, justifications, external circumstances), and moral obligations/demands/ duties expected of the person.

Phase 5: Blame 2

5.1 Expressed judgments of blameworthiness and punishment.
Additional considerations that are not explicitly built into the model.

o If you were the victim of the person's negative act (as opposed to an observer) how would you navigate the phases differently? Perhaps your emotions would likely be stronger and more negative. This might result in a longer delay between perceiving the expectation violation and entering Phase 4. This would partly depend on an individual's ability to regulate emotions ("self-sooth").

o Sometimes people talk about being angry at the event but not at the person. Does this seem like the result of moving through Phase 4 and determining that the person (for a variety of reasons) does not deserve to be blamed and then trying to find an appropriate outlet for your (negative) feelings?

o The greater the event ambiguity, the greater the likelihood of moving to Phase 4?

o The majority of the models we have read do not seem to recognize that blaming someone is a motivated process: Sometimes we want to blame others, and this motivation is not always open to reasoned consideration. Because the models assume that blame assignment is a rational process, they have investigated what factors (generally grouped in our Phase 4 under the heading "Considerations of Responsibility") need to be present before people feel justified in assigning blame to others. It seems more likely that people assess blameworthiness for a variety of reasons (to boost self-esteem, to maintain a sense of controllability around the negative event -- as when people blame victims of violent crimes, to show adherence to a moral system, or even to create group cohesion, as in scapegoating). How would these different motivations impact the way people navigate through the phases? Again, the role of affect and its ability to derail reasoned thinking seems important.
