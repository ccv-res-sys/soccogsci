Week 4 (April 17): Research on Responsibility

We began with some disparaging remarks about the published studies on responsibility and blame:

  1. Studies often use vignettes exclusively.
  2. This means there is little room for the affect associated with real blameworthy events. As a result people are often starting at Phase 4 and appear more rational than they are.
  3. Vignettes often describe very large violations as opposed to small violations, which are probably more common.
  4. Studies generally place the participant in the role of an observer as opposed to someone who is afflicted by the negative act (which is probably the situation in which most of us begin to allocate blame).
  5. Researchers often seem to always prefer the use of vignettes over actually asking participants to reflect on real conflicts between themselves and another person
  6. Studies often use a fixed order of questions that, not so surprisingly, perfectly reconstruct the temporal and "causal" links in the proposed models.
In light of the above criticisms, we decided to devise studies that test our own model, as sketched out in Week 3.

Aim: To explore how people naturally move through the various phases of "moral perception" and the extent to which individual differences moderate this progression. In particular, we were interested in how often people actually move to Phase 4 or whether many people just stop at Phase 3 ("Blame 1").

In both studies (see below) we were interested in recording facial expressions, physiological measures of arousal, and self-report data on thoughts and feelings. Additionally, we were interested in exploring any individual differences that might significantly impact people's blame allocation processes. Some potentially important individual differences are need for cognition (how much people typically invest to figure things out, such as event categorization and causality), emotional reactivity, and propensity to feel anger or sadness.

The Cookie Study

Participants interact with a confederate who eats one of their cookies (or spills water on their lap).

During the interaction, participants will be unobtrusively videotaped. (We might also monitor their physiological arousal or ask them to record their emotions using a hand-held device. In this case, a cover story would have to introduce the recording devices for a credible purpose.)

In order to assess their thought and emotional processes we could:

  1. Interview them about their thoughts/feelings
  2. Ask them to report on their thoughts and feelings as they review a videotape of the cookie fiasco
Additional Manipulations: We were curious about how the level of intimacy between the norm violator and the participant would impact people's blame allocation process. And we questioned how cognitive load would impact how participants moved through the different phases. Our model would predict that a "narrow" cognitive space would short-circuit some of the phases and make the person more vulnerable to default assumptions, quick blame expression, fewer (re)considerations of responsibility, etc.

Computer Game

Participants would play a videogame in which there are rule violations or expectation violations (such as an expectation for fairness or collaboration).

We would assess their thoughts and feelings using the methods above. In addition, we could monitor acts of aggression (during the game) that might be interpretable as expressions of Blame 1. A think-aloud method would be particularly appropriate for this study type.

Additional Manipulations: We were interested in varying the intimacy between the participant and other player(s). We also wanted to see what happened if participants were led to expect that they would or would not meet the other participants.

As a side note, it might be feasible to actually do a form of this study by joining forces with Holly Arrow. She is currently developing a computer game that examines group formation.
