Week 2 (April 3): Classic Readings

The class identified the following central issues in the readings:
  • Virtue, pleasure and pain as understood differently in Aristotle and Hume

  • Defining/understanding authors' views of responsibility & intentionality

  • The function of virtues and vices in Hume

  • Aristotle's treatment of ethics as ethos/habit

  • The non-universal nature of virtues: Which virtues and for whom?

  • Hume's understanding of the origin & nature of moral sentiments

  • Hume's understanding of necessity & inference in the perceiver

  • Hume's understanding of motives & actions in the agent

  • What can moral agents control?

Virtue

  • Aristotle argues that associations between certain moral actions and the experience of pleasure or pain must be trained - an area for education

  • Aristotle's view is that actions toward the right ends must be practiced in order for an agent to learn to see them as right and develop the virtue as a habit

  • Aristotle lays out the classic mitigating circumstances for responsibility assignments: ignorance and external compulsion. However, he allows for ignorance as mitigating responsibility only if the agent feels some compunction to correct his/her ignorance.

  • Hume emphasizes the (societal) utility of acts as the standard for measuring it to be praiseworthy or blameworthy; this utility is perceived both by the agent and by the perceiver or recipient of the act.
Questions remaining

- What happens when an agent's/perceiver's dispositions regarding pain and pleasure clash with the societal standards (perhaps because the person has not been educated well)? How can one assign responsibility in this case? Read this article from a death row inmate to get a feeling for such a clash, and its problems for assigning responsibility.

- What happens, in Hume's model, when perceiver and agent have different perceptions of utility?

Function of moral sentiments, according to Hume

  • The person possessed of virtue finds useful and agreeable that which is also useful and agreeable to society

  • Societal mores depend on the psychology of both the agent and respondent. Both act/respond consistent with an "enlightened egoism." These dispositions interact and create the societal emergence of an innate tendency to find the societally useful things attractive.

  • Moral sentiments are human constructions; reliably brought about by the perception of actions and their motives.

  • Causation is a human construction, according to Hume. Nevertheless, his theory seems to assume an "objective" causal process according to which moral sentiments are reliably ("necessarily") triggered by actions and motives.

  • Broader discussion of the need for "believing in objective causality." A Humean scientist can pragmatically "do her job" without much concern for semantic/rhetorical questions of "knowledge" or "truth." Yet, it is not clear what this means when science is called on to provide evidence in law or data for policy-making (e.g., educational policy, environmental policy).
