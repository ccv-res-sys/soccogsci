Week 7 (May 8): Intrapersonal moral sentiments

Guilt

  • being guilty comes from doing (act or omission) that violates some standard
  • there is always comparative reference to a value base
  • virtually if not always moral
  • there is reference to a victim
  • per Williams, the victim is often empowered to become (angry) enforcer of the norm, and the guilty actor fears the enforcer
  • developmentally, the parent embodies the standard/norm, functions as the "victim "(it is the parent’s norms that are violated) and also the enforcer; modeling and teaching leads to internalization of the norm as well as the anger of violating it -- guilt then is self-directed anger
  • feeling guilt requires some act that harms and/or is evaluated as a norm violation, but it is unclear whether it requires that the actor be responsible for the act; feeling "guilty by association" (for something a close or subordinate other did) may be learned as an extension of genuine guilt
  • guilt is empathy related: the actor empathizes with the victim when feeling guilty
  • developmentally, the guilt is stronger in children whose parents have been warm and affectionate (why? perhaps because parents who are warm are also empathic and teach the child empathy, then leading to more guilt-capability)
  • guilt is a cognitively elaborated emotion, based on agitation/fear/anxiety (per Ferguson); the anxiety is over consequences of the guilty act: victim anger, character implications, possible punishment
  • despite the forward-looking anxiety emphasis (Lifton has called guilt the "anxiety of responsibility"), there is a retrospective aspect as well: guilt involves an element of regret (over doing or not-doing something), a "should have" or "should not have"
  • it is association between guilt and punishment that makes more research on intrapunitive aspects of guilt warranted; Baumeister’s coinlcusion that dismisses this connection may be premature
  • guilt is often followed by confession, apology and reparation by the guilty one (community ethics requires mitigation of harm, willingness to make amends)

Shame
  • in Williams’ root metaphor, the dynamic of shame is of the target being fully exposed, being seen naked (even being seen through) by a watcher, who is at the same time an evaluator
  • Feeling shame is more directed at "being a certain way" – one’s character rather than one’s actions. Even if triggered by an action, the object of one’s shame is the action’s implication of a certain flaw of character.
  • in shame, there is no victim, as in guilt
  • however, as in guily, shame always involves reference to a standard that is not met (typically one’s own desire to have a moral or valued character)
  • the standards can be moral or nonmoral (Sabini & Silver talk about aesthetic standards)
  • when moral, if there has been "doing", some action, then there is likely to be a link with guilt: guilty actions would imply a shameful actor more than a single guilty act;
  • vicarious shame or shame by associated seem more frequent, and easier to trigger, than vicarious guilty feelings (and perhaps feeling guilty for another’s act is strictly possible only when one could and should have prevented that act)
  • nonmoral shame can be invoked simply by failing to meet a social standard or expectation, including reciprocity, merit, etc. (e.g., the one who gets a huge salary raise without feeling that it was fully deserved will feel some shame, but also some guilt – see below)
  • so the fundamental elements of shame are: being the focus of attention (watched) and being concerned about a flawed disposition in onself, relative to some standard
  • the watcher/evaluator can be actual, imagined, or internalized
  • most often, an actual watcher/evaluator is indeed devaluing the target in a way that is global, that rejects the whole person
  • but sometime only the shameful target feels that the other is devaluing; the evaluator may not actually be critical, but the potential for evaluation, for discovery of a flaw may suffice to trigger shame
  • if the watcher is internal, there is even more clearly a devaluing occurring
  • the standards/values according to which evaluation takes place are special ones: they are ones that are highly prized by the target of the shaming
  • the devaluation of the shaming experience generalizes from the specific exposed flaw to the whole person (low self-esteem experience)
  • in the ashamed person, there is a discrepancy between the actual and the desired, and such discrepancy (per Higgins) leads to dejection-based emotions. There is a variation of discrepancy that occurs when the target person receives benefits that he or she does not deem equitable, deserved – the resultgin complex feeling may include guilt (for not having done what would have made the benefit deserving), shame (for being exposed, but in a flawed way)
  • the discrepancy between actual and desired self is a loss from and of the self, and hence a loss of self-esteem; shame is linked then to depression spectrum emotions, including disappointment; also to embarrassment

Difficult Cases

Victim guilt

victim feels guilty because person believes that he or she could and should have acted differently (to prevent the event); victim is sometimes blamed by others

victim is concerned about not staying in control, can easily overestime potential control and therefore feels regret/guilt for omitted acts of prevention

Survivor guilt and thriver (positive inequity) guilt:

Probably a combination of guilt and shame:

genuine guilt feelings due to empathy for victims (or non-thrivers) and for not preventing others’ demise (lack of thriving);

shame feelings because of receiving attention, being exposed; but not feeling "deserving" – this discrepancy (relative to equity or reciprocity norms) makes self appear flawed. "why me?", "who am I to survive this/get this out of proportion reward?" No justification may be possible, which leads to depressed feelings of undeservingness, characteristic of shame

Pride

Is it the inverse of guilt or shame?

Pride is usually directed at achievement or prosocial actions, less often at character strength (feeling good about oneself generally is typically just called high self-esteem or self-confidence)

Pride can be quite private (just as guilt can), but public attention to positive act increases feelings of pride (just as it does for shame)

similar structure for moral pride: there is a "benefacted" (instead of a victim) who via praise and gratitude is an "enforcer" of the relevant standards the action meets or exceeds; pride then becomes internalized praise

Critique of Empirical Studies on Guilt/Shame

Most studies try to distinguish between guilt and shame, but conclusions about the "maladaptiveness" of shame seem premature: Studies are limited methodologically (typically scenarios or porjective tests), correlations are overinterpreted as causation (e.g., the person who feels shame may feel that as a response to maladaptive life events, not as the cause of those events). In Bandura et al.’s work, too, causation is inferred from pure correlations, and the role of moral disengagement may well be a result of repeated immoral acts (a form of post-hoc ratinalization) and not their cause
