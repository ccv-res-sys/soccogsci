Self-Concept: Introduction (Apr 2)

Basic Concepts and Distinction

  • Self - Self-Concept

    These two terms are sometimes used interchangeably; however, the first really refers to the person (who decides, perceives, thinks, and acts), whereas the second refers to a representation of oneself. The self-concept was defined in class as the sum of all your thoughts and feelings about yourself.

  • Self-Concept - Self-Description

    The self-concept cannot be measured directly; we use various forms of self-description tasks to measure it (e.g., spontaneous answer to "who are you?"; adjective rating scales, yes-no responses to brief computer-presented adjectives, etc.)

  • Self-Concept - Self-Views

    Self-perceptions in specific domains are usually called "self-views" (e.g., the view of yourself in academics, or your thoughts about your extraversion). The sum of all these specific views is then the self-concept.

  • The "Schema" Organization

    This topic is covered in detail in the Malle & Horowitz article. A schema, as defined in class, is a network of interassociated representations stored in memory. Recall the metaphor of a network of small lamps into which current is sent at one point; as soon as that happens, several lamps light up because of their interconnection. The tighter these connections, the less resistance there is, and the longer the current travels before dissipating.

  • "Affect" in the self-concept

    The self-concept does not only contain beliefs, thoughts, and memories about oneself, but also feelings -- most of the articles in your reading for Apr 7 touch on this "affective" aspects of the self-concept.

Affect in the Self-Concept

  • Domain-Specific Effects vs. Global Effects

    Affect for specific self-views is covered by Malle & Horowitz , who compare positive self-views to negative self-views. Self-esteem, by contrast, is the feeling of worth one has about oneself as a whole -- not just in a particular domain. Dutton & Brown discuss the relationship between specific self-views and self-esteem in their article.

  • Self-Image Maintenance

    Clearly, people try to achieve a fairly positive image of themselves and also try to portray such a positive image for others. I mentioned a few examples of "standard self serving biases" that have been described in the literature (e.g., taking large credit for one's housework contributions; trying to deflect responsibility for accidents).

    Activities to "maintain" one's global self-esteem or more specific feelings of self-worth in particular domains, are described in the remaining articles for Apr 7.

    • "self-love" -- the tendency to like the things associated with oneself better, such as the letters or one's name (Kitayama & Karasawa)

    • self-affirmation -- the tendency to "distract" oneself from failures in one domain by affirming one's worth in another domain (briefly described in (Fein & Spencer)

    • derogating others -- the tendency to increase one's own self-worth by putting down others. Fein & Spencer explore under what conditions people show this trend.

    • aggrandizing others -- the tendency to make another person look particularly good or competent when derogating him or her would not work (Alicke et al.)
