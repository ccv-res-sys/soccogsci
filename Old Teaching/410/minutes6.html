Minutes of Discussion on May 12
Perceiving Behavior: Intentions and Intentionality
Prepared by Andrea Raz

  1. Folk concept of intentionality
    • Clearly, people distinguish between intenstions actions. In addition, they also evalaute intentions les extremely (they "discount" them) compared to their corresponding actions. This discounting effect appears to be stronger for positive intentions/actions. One explanation for this effect is that if people see someone have a negative intention they feel they must respond, to protect themselves or even others, and one way of doing it is to blame (see abstract and replciation study of Malle & Bennett, 1998).
    • Even though people agree to a high degree in their judgmnents of intentionality, there are only few behaviors for which 100% of people would assign intentionality, because it is easy to have some doubts (especially as an observer). These doubts are easily available considering beahviors in the athletic or artistic domain, where the skill factor is important, because it is often hard to determine whether a person truly has the skill or not (e.g., 50-foot basket; hole-in-one).
    • Recognition of others' intentions are so important because people don't always follow through with intentions and attempts to influence them are worth while. For example, through blame and counteractions we have a chance to prevent a negative intention from becoming action.
    • Not all aspects of an action are intentional. Sometimes we have to step back and rely on our bodies to execute a given intention (e.g., opening the car door). It would be too difficult to intend to open the door exactly in one particular way.
    • Should we call all actions intentional? perhapos not. Something can be an action without having been based on an intention, so it may be called accidental action (e.g., you accidentally call your mom rather than your brother).
    • Most bodily reflexes occur so fast and without the person's control that they are entirely unintentional. However, they may once have been intentional actions, at the time one learned them (e.g., a Karate move).
    • Some behaviors may not hold intentionality but the person who performs them may still be held responsible for the outcome of that behavior. For example, when a person is drunk and harms someone in this state, there may be no intentionality but still (legal and moral) responsibilty because the person decided (i.e., intended) to get drunk in the first place.

  2. The concept of intention may be missing in schizophrenic patients. Their own action control system is disturbed -- they cannot easily have a desire, form an intention, and act on it, because their minds follow incidental trails that distract and confuse them. In parallel, their system of interpreting actions and intentions of others is disturbed.
