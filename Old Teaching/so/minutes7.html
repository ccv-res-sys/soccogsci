A copy of the SCS was given to each member of the seminar and its use and history was discussed. Discussion of public self-awareness versus private self-awareness ensued. One's self-awareness is unique when viewed from an internal perspective. The view of the other is limited to external features. One question was when does increasing self-awareness result in negative affect? There were several ideas, one being that increased self-awareness may focus on negative features of self, another than self-awareness makes discrepancies between ideal standards and current states more salient.

Another point made was that individuals don't always try to reduce those discrepanices -- they don't always try to reduce dissonance. Sometimes positive thought substitution (self-affirmation) is more effective and simply avoids dissonant focus. It was noted that when individuals are in a laboratory environment, which is sterile, unfamiliar, and impoverished in terms of self affirming accouterments, such as photos of friends, trophies, awards, and degrees, more dissonant effects may be evident. In the lab, subjects may be more inclined to ruminate about self-discrepancies.

The discussion turned to the question of whether self-affirmation is as good as other-affirmation. It was suggested that when negative feedback comes from an external source, the affirmation also needs to be from another person in order to fully offset a negative self-perception. It was then pointed out that some negative self-beliefs are very powerful and not assuaged by the positive affirmations of others.

On a tangent relating to the pleasure (or not) of self-awareness, it was suggested that if one were to put introverts into a room for 15 minutes and compare their mood outcome with extroverts placed into the same conditions, the introverts would likely rate higher in positive mood and extroverts would probably be more anxious. The reason seems to be that introverts are more likely to entertain themselves with internal processed than the extroverts, who would be more likely to look to the external environment for interaction.

The question was raised of how to distinguish between public, social, and private self. It was suggested that two questions need to be asked: (1) Which self-features are represented? (content of representation) Features can be called "private" if they are normally only knowable by the person him- or herself, such as private thoughts or feelings. Features can be called "public" is they are publicly knowable, such as facial expressions, body shape, or actions. (2) Who is considered to represent those features? (perspective) The person may represent private or public self-features from the self-perspective, such as worrying about mood his own fluctuations or body weight. The person may also represent those features from an other-perspective, worrying about how others perceive her mood fluctuations or body weight. Theese two different questions clarify some confusion in the debate on the private-public distinction and help derive hypotheses about the behavioral consequences of self-awareness.
