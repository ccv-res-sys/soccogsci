11/11: Self-Perception

We began with the questions: when does the self regard the self as an "other" might regard it? How can the self be unclear to itself? When do we not have privileged knowledge of ourselves?

This led to a distinction between knowledge of a *current state* (which we do have access to) and attribution of that state to a cause, which can be mistaken. As stated in the Bem article, cues allowing attribution might be missing or unclear. It was additionally pointed out, however, that we might be unable to explain *where* such cues arose from, even if we were *aware* that they were there...that is, we might have a mistaken belief, based not on features of the cues, but on an implicit theory, a la Ross.

Continuing on the Ross idea, lack of memory itself might cause greater reliance on implicit theories to account for all our personal data. This gives us a "source monitoring" problem -- not only might we lack the ability to correctly attribute data to source, we lack even a confident memory representation.

There ensued a discussion of the effects of present state in biasing construction of reports of past memories. Would, for instance, perception of past pain be influenced by current pain level? ..by possession of an implicit theory? How could this be tested, given the lack of objective comparisons for pain? One suggestion was to manipulate implicit theories, and observe changes in reported pain.

This led into some developmental literature: that satiated kids deny their prior state of hunger. Children seem to have no knowledge of how they achieve their beliefs. This idea was explored, and then the application of this information to the initial questions was briefly discussed.

Returning to the initial questions: it was pointed out that cues can be weak or ambiguous in other ways. Split-brain patients, for instance, as mentioned in Elster, don't know the *real* reasons for their actions but come up with impressive justifications. It was suggested that there might even be something like an "interpreter" (Ira Black) (the progenitor of this idea, not the interpreter) in the brain that supplies explanations. This led to an account of an allegedly far-fetched idea of Dennett's: that dreams are our interpretations given to the images generated by the brain's random firings, forming Rorschach blots entirely of our own creation. This was also compared to the propensity of people to generate hypotheses and "meaning in randomness," as studied especially in the field of judgment and decision-making. Indeed, it may be better to do this and have a high false alarm rate than to reject all possible hypotheses too soon.

It was pointed out that we do this with hopes as well as hypotheses, which generated some discussion about motivational biases. Hope, though, is palliative and, generally, something for which we are non-accountable -- in contrast to beliefs. Switching topics, there was a general call for questions. A question was asked about the Wilson paper, and the ability of people to nullify the effects of priming when the manipulation is known -- for instance, when the tendency to feel bad because of poor weather is separated into the questions of "how's the weather?" and "how do you feel?". This was contrasted with Robert Mauro's "laugh track" example -- people still find situations paired with it funnier, even when they know why this is so. Emotional contagion was suggested as a reason.

It was suggested that, in looking at reasons-analysis studies, it is necessary to consider both what knowledge was accessible and what was asked for -- these are not the same things. We're very good at accessing how we feel...but this doesn't necessarily predict our behavior. If someone asks why you feel a certain way, you might generate a causal history of how that state came to be, or you might *construct* a justification. In the latter case, there's no privileged access...and when we do have privileged access, we don't show distortions due to reasons analysis. Experts, for instance, have this kind of access -- they give reasons more akin to histories instead of justifications, and they don't show reasons analysis distortions. It's as if experts stroll within their "attitudinal structure" when generating their reasons rather than just going in "feeling space." The answers they generate fit their actions, because their judgments are based on their reasons. For non-experts, when reasons are no longer accessible, they return to this pure affect node...and are later dissatisfied with actions taken on the basis of these momentarily constructed reasons.

This led to the infamous "dating couples" study: those who analyzed their reasons for being together were less likely to still be together when a follow-up was conducted. In the less traumatic "poster study", Ss found that reasons for preferring cheezy posters were easier to generate than reasons for preferring art prints; the follow-up found those who analyzed their reasons didn't like their posters as much as those who didn't analyze.

Doesn't this show a total failure of dissonance reduction? Not necessarily -- what was the cost involved? Simply *not* getting the other poster? It would be interesting to know whether Ss in the follow-up had an actual preference for the poster not chosen. Dissonance reduction could be said to have taken place if Ss switch their focus to the negative aspects of the not-chosen options.

The process involved in the "reasons-analysis regret effect" was spelled out: people use their "affect node" to give the first answer. Then they engage in reasons analysis. Then they report their attitudes, based on those reasons. In dating couples, those reasons analyses set in motion behavior that led to the couples' breakups. In order to know *exactly* what happened in this case, or whether those couples experienced regret as a result of this study, we'd need to know all the intervening steps...there's a lot of paths that action resulting from these reasons analyses could take; the breakups could have occurred at any juncture.

How is it, then, if reasons fade and initial affect returns, that attitudes are ever changed? In therapy, for example, reasons given to justify attitudes (and presumably attitude changes) can sound wonderful in session, but fade for the client after leaving... This problem was compared to Lowenstein's work on the "visceral self" -- that pallid discussions of, say, a drug addict's knowledge of her probable behavior once withdrawal sets in, are insufficiently powerful compared with the visceral experience of *being* in that situation.

Of course, in therapy, the lack of change might also arise because the *real* cause of the attitude is, perhaps motivationally, inaccessible. Even experts may just be giving post-hoc "reasons" consistent with their affect. This led to the question of whether experts, in fact, *do* give more reports of causal histories in their reasons. This, in turn, led to the question of whether reasons change the framework of interpretation -- that is, change implicit theories. It was decided that this can, in fact, happen, especially if the reason the person gives is "I have this attitude because I am this type of person" -- in effect, what they're doing is changing their self-schema...and this might be useful in addressing the problem of attitude change within therapy.

Another way of manipulating self-perception via reasons is Norbert Schwarz' technique of providing a scale with the appropriate skew: for example, asking participants for twelve behavioral instantiations of an attitude will make it more difficult for them to comply, and hence may cause them to question the strength of their attitude in this domain. Of course, their initial affect will still recur. This led to some disagreement: on the one hand, affect ought to be more easily conditionable than cold cognition; on the other hand, it was felt that affect was particularly enduring.

This "conditioning" notion (I think) led to another discussion of the "couple breakup" scenario and the role of Gricean implicature: what are the effects of repeatedly asking a question? This is normally done to resolve confusion or as a challenge. It was mentioned in support of this that peer counselors are instructed never to ask "why" questions, and that some -- but not all -- schools of therapy also discourage the practice. The reasons the different clinical theories proffer for their positions was briefly discussed.

What are the effects, then, of repeated requests to engage in reasons analysis over time? It was established that this did not *necessarily* lead to polarized beliefs, unless the only accessible thoughts were highly consistent. What happens when someone is repeatedly asked to give reasons, and no new information is given to them? It was suggested that this might result in shifts in either direction -- either a drive to rationalize (and hence boost) one's liking for the thing being questioned about, or in self-doubt. In the "couples" study, however, this shift was unidirectional. It was agreed that this was an area in which further investigation was needed.

The asymmetry in the kinds of behavioral events that people explain was also pointed out. We are asked "why" questions about our actions..but not our experiences. We don't ask ourselves why we hold our attitudes; rather, attitudes are used *as* explanations. However, we do embed our attitudes in implicit theories. If, while analyzing reasons, you generate negatives about your partner, but hold a theory of relationships that acknowledges that, as the saying goes, negatives occur, your attitudes will be in effect inoculated against change. These theories thus seem also to carry some explanatory weight. This might be disadvantageous in therapy, where focusing on making alternative sorts of reasons available might merely teach the client how better to tolerate ambiguity.

There was a general intellectual lull and some discussion about a break.

Pressing on, we tackled the Nisbett and Valins notion that couching explanations in situational terms might be a more successful and pleasant strategy. Indeed, it was pointed out, therapy often tries to teach this switch -- couching failures in situational terms and successes in dispositional ones. The starting point of this tradition was then immediately and impressively reclaimed for attribution theory: Weiner, Seligman, Peterson contributed the notions of learned helplessness, the dimensions of stable/unstable, controlled/uncontrolled to clinical practice. The history of the person/situation debate was discussed...historically, attributions to dispositions have been perceived more negatively; the Nisbett article is consistent with this trend in pointing out the beneficial use of situational attributions. Of course, it also needs to be recognized that it's important to attribute *positive* qualities of oneself to dispositions. There may be an interesting gender effect here: women have (or had, we weren't sure) a greater tendency to attribute successes to situational factors; the authors who urged "balance" and the tonic effects of situational attributions were male.

It was then explained that the "catalog" of attributional biases seems inconsistent (e.g., self-serving bias and actor-observer asymmetry). This, it was suggested (graphically) was the result of not studying the entire range of factors which can be subsumed in the judgment attributed to person or situation -- that is, not recognizing that valence as well as type of behavior can vary. There was brief discussion as to whether greater situational attributions might lead to a diminution of personal responsibility, and possibly even an increase in criminal activity. It seems unlikely that attribution would play much of a role, given that attribution tendencies vary cross-culturally (Miller, 1984), and crime patterns don't track this variation. It was argued that the confounds in this table need to be separated -- different puzzle pieces have been studied but no integration as yet achieved of valence, behavior type, and status as actor or observer, etc.

The question arose of how personality dimensions fit into this picture. Some of attribution theory, it was answered, is an offshoot of Rotter's work on locus of control. This, apparently, however, was a bad scale, leading to inconsistent results in the literature. Clinical scales have provided better measurement of attributional style. Interestingly, studies on the "control" dimension found that people who attribute bad things to their own characteristics may benefit from the increased feelings of control.

This led to a discussion of how one might "benefit" from negative dispositional attributions to oneself. One explanation might be that much of what gets called "dispositional" -- with the implication of stability -- might better be called "other person causes," implying that it comes from within but is not stable. Examples of these might be desires or goals, which are both changeable but also internal. On the other hand, according to Freyd's theory, given the need to maintain a stable relationship with an abusive person, it might behoove the other party to indeed make a stable dispositional conclusion of "I am a bad person." Of course, any situation in which someone would need to do this would be negative, and it's hard to determine whether the situation or its putative remedy is actually worse. Then too, attribution theory has a normative basis, whereas Freyd's theory is (or should be) descriptive -- not what a person *should* do to maintain such a relationship, but rather, what they actually do. Outcome research would be needed to make prescriptive claims.

It was asked whether affect changes when attributions or related cognition changes (e.g., in therapy). Gilbert's notion of cognitive "metastasis" was discussed: any cognition resulting from any given attribution would set in motion such a cascade of effects that the correction of a single piece of the belief chain would surely be insufficient to erase the diffuse effects. Alternative methods of changing cognition were discussed. A depressed person, for instance, might be unable to ferret out all the connections to the depressed ideation but might be able to build a parallel set of (more pleasant) mental structures to use. Of course, there'd be individual differences in the efficacy of this solution -- and, it was noted, for depressed patients who did this, re-activation of the depressive schemas caused relapse to the use of the initial structures. Human Subjects approval for studies on theses issues was discussed. Class concluded with the question of whether Bertram believed in the brain (he held his head in an ambiguous gesture). Readings for next week were previewed and declared "cute," "classic", and "not puffed-up."
