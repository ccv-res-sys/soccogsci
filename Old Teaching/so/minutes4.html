10/28: Relationships and Attachment

The conceptual framework of today's topic:

DIFFERENTIATION: How do people differentiate parts of themselves? Is this a theory of mind ability? Examples in adults: self deception; multiple selves.

RELATIONSHIPS: How do models of relationships represent self and other? Also, when do self and other relationships go awry? Examples of self/other differentiation: Shapiro article--hysterical style (the perfect empathizer?); Jack article--taking on a specific "other" role. What kind of self representation or stability does one need to make relationships work? What is a good mix of self/other representations for successful relationships?

Q: How does the Elster article fit in?

R: Elster highlights subsystems of the self that operate like self/other, yet all of this happens inside the self. His article foreshadows the idea of "satellites of the self," which is the topic of next week's seminar (Tuesday, November 5).

The role of a theory of mind:

How does a theory of mind help you understand others? Theory of mind may give you the flexibility to understand others and to create alternative representations of the self. Autistic individuals may not be the only ones with deficits in a theory of mind (ToM). Sociopaths--people with "no self"--may be taking ToM to the extreme. This idea of having "not enough" or "too much" of a ToM--is there a continuum of ToM? Are the various ToM abilities (e.g., false belief, appearance-reality, perspective-taking) tapping into the same ability? How can we test this ToM dimension(s) in adults? The classic failures children exhibit in false-belief tasks seem to appear in adults who are under a high cognitive load. There is a distinction between having the structure--the theory--and applying it to situations. Example: "Silencing the Self"--it is not that the women don't understand false belief or don't have a ToM. Perhaps society only supports one perspective, so for these women to be in a healthy relationship, there would need to be a "representational change"--a recognition that somebody has a false belief.

Pretense as an example of deviating from reality:

Pretend play in children is clearly marked as a deviation from reality (e.g., "we're just pretending, right?"). In pretense, there is only one true reality and the pretense is definitely NOT real; pretense is much easier to grasp than other deviations from reality (such as perspective taking). When you must deal with two different perspectives on the same reality (two versions of reality), things get tricky. Example: hostile media phenomenon. Two groups in opposition (e.g., Israelis and Palestinians) view the same piece of neutral media coverage as biased in favor of the other group. Each group is so certain that their perspective (their version of the truth) is RIGHT that any deviation from that perspective is read as hostile. Comment: Generating a parallel reality is much more difficult than generating an opposite one.

Imaginary Companions (ICs) in children & adults:

Who is in control of our imaginary companions? Do children know or acknowledge that they are in control of their IC? Maybe not explicitly, but children will often deny somebody else trying to control their IC. Example of the adult version of IC: Novelists (fiction writers) whose characters "take over" the story writing. Does this occurrence/phenomenon weaken the "true" self? Yes. The link between novelists and both alcoholism and depression.

Multiple Selves and Multiple Personality Disorder (MPD):

What is going on with cases of multiple selves? Are the other selves aware of one another? Are they "selves"? Who is in control? Don't think of other selves as agents. Think of them as alternative representations of the true agent--the one who makes sense of everyone and is the "recipient" of all the other representations. Multiple Personality Disorder: It is not always the case that the "true" self--the "interpretive system"--is aware of or in control of the other selves operating out there. Is there an extreme, extreme case in which there are multiple interpretive systems? Not in MPD, and probably not at all.

Less extreme examples: "Different scripts"

Attachment styles differ with different people, suggesting that we employ different scripts in different situations. Also, we use different speech styles (diction, pace, accent) when interacting with different people. How do we integrate them all? They aren't really "separate selves" but they become more different and more distinct as you deny the different scripts and refuse to let them crossover.

Back to MPD and ICs:

Q: Are alternate selves = ICs?

R: Yes and no. "Yes" because illogical, impossible things can happen with alternate personalities that make them seem totally separate from the "true" self (as are ICs). There may be a man personality in a woman's body and the man will deny that this is the case. There may be a suicidal personality who denies that suicide would kill the other personalities. "No" because ICs don't have a physical self at all (as do multiple personalities). Also, ICs can't do all the things that alternative personalities can do because adults have ToM abilities that go well beyond children's limited understanding.

Q: Switching Selves---Multiple Selves---Imaginary Companions: How are these three things related?

R: These are different manifestations of a set of tools the mind has available including a network of representations. Representations can include accents, expressions, body movements, as well as selves. The mind is a network of representations--pathology occurs when there are huge walls between connected representations. What's nice about this idea is that pathology can occur even in the presence of a ToM--there are other places where things can go awry--roadblocks between connected representations, etc.

Distinction between multiple personalities and theory of mind:

A theory of mind seems necessary for having multiple personalities. Could an autistic individual ever have MPD?

An Evolutionary Perspective:

Social interaction is what we, as humans, are designed for. Perhaps ICs and pretend play give us great practice and provide the necessary groundwork for developing a ToM (like babbling sets the stage for the development of language).
