10/21: Empathy and Perspective Taking

The assigned articles for week four tackled the topic and definition of empathy from different angles. How are these definitions similar and how do they differ? (Hodges and Wegner: automatic vs. controlled empathy; Mendoza: situational vs. emotional empathy; Wispe: sympathy vs. empathy). Also, is there a distinction between cognitive and emotional empathy?

Hodges and Wegner: The simplest example of automatic empathy is emotional and occurs quickly without prior intention. May be invoked by environmental cues. Controlled empathy is more often cognitive empathy (perspective taking?), which is effortful and difficult to achieve. The success of controlled empathy is dependent on the individual's ability to find cues to the experience that will induce the empathic state of mind.

The Davis compares two methods of effortful empathy: One method is to think of one of your past experiences which were similar to what the other person seems to feel (perhaps this leads to automatic empathy?); the other is to just think of the other person's experience.

The Davis article implies that we sometimes (and successfully) use the self as a resource of empathy, by creating analogous situations and determining how we would feel and act during this experience. In the Mendoza article, this is termed situational empathy, in which the target to be imagined is the situation (the circumstances) and only by implication how we would act and feel.

In class, a possible artifact of the Mendoza study was addressed. Because the couples used in this study were arguing, successful situational empathy may have been prevented by a hurt ego and biased perspective. But this fact may make the emotional empathy induction all the more valuable, because it allows one to go back to one's own past experiences (thus not denying the self"), thus momentarily leaving the injured ego state and arriving at the other person's perspective.

The Batson article found women show more empathy if they had shared the experience of the target, while men do not increase their level of empathy. Research (by Nolen-Hoeksema) has shown that women discuss their problems with each other and try to relate with the other's experience, forming a bond. This act of empathizing actually makes the women discussing the situation feel worse. Men on the other hand, generally don't deal with the problem internally, but externally. They generally are solution oriented and limit the level of empathy.

The men's refusal to make use of their own pertinent experiences may explain the Batson results. Another possible explanation may be a self report discrepancy. Men may be repressing or regulating their self-reported scores of empathy (otherwise they might get too upset, which is socially discouraged; see Gottman & Levenson's work). Similar to Davis and Mendoza, Batson concludes that it is not necessary to have a shared experience to accurately empathize. In fact, Mendoza states a shared experience may even interfere with empathy if the individual considers the experience and insists that he/she acted or would have acted differently.

In class, it was argued that the individual does not have to experience the full blown feeling at the time to empathize: conceptual memory vs. emotional memory. Is conceptual memory really empathy (reflecting on the feeling without feeling the feeling)? How close does one have to match to empathize?

Distinction between empathy and sympathy:
Sympathy = feelings of tenderness; heightened awareness of someone's suffering, which creates the urge to alleviate their suffering. Motivated by feeling moved, and brought on by less effort than empathy. In sympathy, the self and other are separate and distinct. It is more an act of compassion than comprehension or mimicry. It was discussed in class whether the sympathizer needs to share the experiences with the target person or whether a "general" compassion suffices.
Empathy: attempt of the self-aware self to comprehend the perspective of the target. In empathy, the self is the "model" to understanding the other. Unlike sympathy, the urge is to comprehend, not alleviate. Empathy is effortful, and a state of agreement is not necessary.

Altruism: Commonly altruism and thoughts about the self are dichotomous. Davis and Mendoza dispute this claim. One method of becoming altruistic is to go back to the self and empathize. Empathy may be a possible precursor to altruism, and therefore, self-focus and altruism may be more closely related than previously thought.
