10/14: The Self as Reference:
Simulation and False Consensus

Theory Theory versus Simulation Theory

Both theories attempt to answer the question "How do I know what is in another person's mind?"

The debate over which theory provides a better account is relevant with respect to development, but not at the adult level. That is, it seems clear that adults use both theory of mind and simulation to understand and predict others; however, it is still an open question as to how this ability develops. Another way to frame the question is to ask: is simulation enough to get the process going (to understand other's beliefs and knowledge), or do we need some implicit theory to organize the information we have?

Theory Theory (TT) is a scientific theory about the implicit theory individuals use to think about, explain, and make predictions about others' beliefs, desires, knowledge (theory of mind). In essence, this is a scientific theory about folk psycholo gy (FP, a naive theory). TT is a representational theory of mind insofar as it assumes that we form representations of our beliefs, desires, etc., rather than just holding them (we believe that rather than just we believe).

Simulation Theory (ST) is based on the notion of modeling and proposes that individuals use themselves to extrapolate (simulate) another's experience or individuals project their own beliefs, desire, knowledge onto others in order to approxima te those of the other. In this theory, the individual generates pretend inputs to use to approximate a folk psychology, but doesn't need to posit a FP per se. ST allows that we may have representations of beliefs but we don't have to.

There are various subtypes/schools of each theory and they take different positions on some key issues.

ST has one subtype that postulates "isomorphic projection" or total projection (the model model, putting yourself in another person's place), meaning that I move (project) directly from my belief or state to yours. Another subtype, non-isomorphic project ion, allows for more than total projection, suggesting that we can use the simulation apparatus more consciously, controlling input and assessing output.

The argument was made that the more you move away from the model model (total projection) and can update your understanding and observe the process, the closer you get to theory theory.

One central question for the two theories is what each proposes is the medium or expression of the theory for the individual--is our knowledge about beliefs, desires, etc. expressed in words or images or both? The narrow view of TT holds that we represen t our beliefs about other people in propositional terms; the wider view allows that our representations may be less rule-bound and may be pictorial. ST takes no position as to the form in which information is represented.

Another key issue in the debate about the theories relates to the question of what you need initially or at base to accomplish the task of understanding another's mind. TT argues that in order to simulate you first need to have theoretical concepts--i.e. , to know what it is to have a desire, belief, etc. Simulation is then a tool the individual can use once she has rudimentary concepts. Harris (ST), at least, would argue that in projecting from the self, one doesn't need a theory because the mind is tr ansparent to itself (Cartesianism). That is, if you have a belief you are aware you have it, and that awareness provides a sufficient basis to simulate.

The issue of Cartesianism also relates to the question of whether first person knowledge/experience is privileged over third person knowledge. TT would hold there is no difference between first and third person experience--that whatever you can do for yo urself, you can do for another. ST would argue that it is harder to simulate for another than for yourself, because it involves meta-analysis (although similarity of self and other should mediate difficulty of simulation). The question of awareness is c omplex, because it is hard to pin down what you should have access to if you have a "Cartesian" mind--at what level do you have to be aware of your beliefs and desires (example of study where order influenced people's choice of item picked from shelf).

Another issue is each theory's conceptualization of how the individual's ability to know and predict others develops. TT suggests that there is qualitative change and the child may gain successive fragments of FP (possibly some hardwired) or gain progres sively more powerful insights into epistemic categories. ST proposes that the change in simulation is more quantitative, with increasingly accurate and complete simulation of the conditions of the other person developing over time.

Final integrative discussion about TT and ST and the idea that they may develop in parallel and further the elaboration of each other, i.e., that theory could be refined by information gleaned from simulation, and the subsequent refinement of the theory h elps improve and refine simulation, etc. Also, theory of mind could be conceptualized as a framework which requires specific terms or input and simulation is one way of acquiring input (e.g., to specify which desires or beliefs might motivate a person's behavior in a particular situation). This perspective capitalizes on the generative nature of TT as opposed to ST, where TT is more easily applied by the individual in novel situations.

False Consensus Effect

Sara posed the question of whether FCE refers to the same phenomenon as TT/ST.

A TT response would be that FCE is not the same because FCE has to do with how you apply the theory. An ST response would be that FCE results from running a simulation with too many settings on default--i.e., using too much information from oneself witho ut adjustments for the other's differences.

Summary of 4 mechanisms that might account for FCE:

  • Selective exposure: individual associates with similar others and thinks of them when making estimates of likelihood of behavior
  • Focus of attention: individual fails to consider alternative views of the situation, and fills in open details with information that supports her view
  • Attributional differences: individual believes their behavior is related to situational differences so generalize to others in similar situation
  • Motivation: individual overestimates likelihood of her response in others because it supports appropriateness and rationality of her experience/feelings. In contrast, Dawes argues that the FCE is not an error at all, and that people are making reasonable assessments and following Bayesian principles (i.e., they update their interpretation when they get new data) when they estimate that a greater percentage of others in a group will respond like themselves. That is, Dawes suggests that people use their own experience as one data point and estimate from there, and that this is rational or a good inductive reasoning process--it would be irrational if they didn't assess based on this data that they have.

    An interesting variation on FCE from McFarland & Miller article: participants predicted that others would make the same choices as they themselves did, but their own experience in reaction to the choice would be unique. So, for some things we assume we a re the same as others and for some things (perhaps the most private things?) we assume we are unique. Perhaps we learn to correct for some bias due to experience and we are sensitive to information that other people have (this effect does not support ST).

    Discussion of Ross, et al. article: Bertram suggested that the self-choice and prediction of other person are confounded--perhaps the same variable that affects self-choice affects prediction of other's choice, so self-choice cannot really be assessed as affecting prediction of peer choice. He posed the challenge of designing an experiment that would demonstrate that self-choice determined consensus rather than vice versa.
