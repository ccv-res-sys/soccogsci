Synopsis and Comments on Readings

Meehl's points keep returning to the issue of falsifiability. He is concerned

  1. about how little is implicated in a nonsignificant (or significant, for that matter) group comparison

  2. about the use of an easy way out of failed predictions, namely, to explain it away with methodological problem; and

  3. about the lack of strong theories that would make more daring predictions, whose success or failure would be more informative.

Certainly, there is too much attention paid to p values and too little to what the results really mean for the phenomenon in question. One of the worst examples I have seen was in a paper I reviewed, where the author first selected extreme groups (top and bottom 33% of a distribution of scores) and then performed a t test to show that they differed significantly from each other...

But there is nothing inherently wrong with group comparisons. If your hypothesis is that men and women do (not) differ on, say, leadership potential, then your results will be based on a group comparison that has enormous social implications. Of course, when interpreting a p value of, say, .01 we would need to know the effect size and sample size to gauge what implications this difference really has.

Similarly, there is nothing inherently wrong with p values either. They are one piece of information (telling you how likely you can get a result of given magnitude or larger by chance). But we need other pieces of information too (power, sample size, effect size) to judge the meaning of the result. What is wrong is to stop at p values and think that a hypothesis has been falsified or verified; that an insight has been gained. These latter two issues go far beyond p values (and that's what sutdents need to be taught, even in 302!)

Finally, there is nothing inherently wrong with realizing that one's methodology was flawed and with mistrusting, therefore, one's negative finding. (Reviewers and others do it all the time with positive findings!!). The point is that we should not stop at one study (be it "significant" or "not significant"). Only the accumulation of data can be informative. You would never stop at your first subject's data point and say that your hypothesis is confirmed or disconfirmed. Why should be stop at the first study? Phenomena with high complexity need aggregation on many levels to reveal reliable processes.

Motet Discussion

There were many excellent points made in the Motet discussion, a few of which I would like to highlight or comment on.

  • "Soft" predictions are a function of the level of analysis and the complexity of the question. The more specific the question, the more precise a prediction we are capable of offering.

  • Some "soft" theories (e.g., psychoanalysis) have led to amazing insights into the human mind, even if these theories didn't have strong "explanatory power." That's because one important function of theories is to describe phenomena in a new way, to lay out the contituents of a complex phenomenon. Subsequent theories may then be able to elaborate on this description, identify the exact processes, and make more precise predictions.

  • Psychological theories are rarely mutually exclusive. Because of the complex, multiple determination of human behavior, many different factors and processes contribute to behavior. The fact that there are multiple levels of analysis of behavior also encourages the coexistence of many theories.

  • The "fashion dynamics" of psychological theories (fading in and out rather fast) should be a real concern for our field since it hinders cumulative insight. Mostly, I think, this stems from the tradition of philsophy and early psychology to reward broad theories and novel ideas. The advent of empirical methods did not change much of this reward structure. (Note that in physics, too, theoreticians reach fame more easily than experimentalists.) Academics in psychology are not getting jobs, tenure, respect, or other goodies if they plow their neighbor's field; they need to demarcate their own. Also, the relative ease of running studies in psychology increases the possible output of any one researcher; and that puts further pressures on individuation (and makes it easier to get bored, on the researcher side as well as on the consumer side). Of course, many more factors contribute to this dynamic.
