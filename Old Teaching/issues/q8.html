At the very, very end of Tversky & Kahneman's chapter, after I had begun to despair that human beings are incapable of getting anything right, the authors say that judgment heuristics are highly economical and usually effective. Have Tversky & Kahneman, in all their work in this area, compared the frequency with which people make these biases and mistakes when they use heuristics relative to the frequency with which people are right? I would be much more worried about systematic bias if it took place 50% of the time, say, as compared to 1%. 
    No, T & K were not interested in assessing the
incidence of biases or errors; they wanted to demonstrate
that those biases exist and that they can lead to
demonstrable judgment deficits.  In the late 80s there
have been several strong reactions from researchers who
argued that heuristics-and-bias research doesn't tell us
much about our actual judgment accuracy, just on the
possibilities of being wrong.  See here for some references.
    Also, now I'm intrigued, can we call up Tversky & Gilovich and ask them to please repeat their "hot hand" in basketball analysis on tennis players?

  2. The Kahneman & Tversky article presented very interesting examples of determinants of choice in risky and riskless contexts. However, I had some trouble with certain "Problems" they presented to the subjects. I was wondering how much the subjects' affect to the words used in the problems comes into play when subjects are deciding between two situations within the problem. In Problem 2, for example, to say that people will "die" may not be exactly juxtaposed to people will be "saved" (Problem 1). "Die" may elicit such negative emotions that the first situation in Problem 2 may not be chosen merely because of the order in which it came (first). 
    The order of presentation is counterbalanced in well-done
studies.  But you point to an imnportant element of
framing: the very associations people have for words
describing the decision problem can influence the decision
people make.

  4. I have been struck with the resistance to change, both in relation to stereotyping and judgment/decision-making. Does this suggest that so-called "top-down" processing has much greater power than "bottom-up" processing? Could the strength of the heuristics which we use for everyday decision-making be part of the problem of resisting disconfirming information re: breaking down stereotypes?
    Higgins & Bargh (1987; assigned for lecture 6)
devote a section of their article to the comparison of
top-down and bottom-up processing.  The power and ease of
top-down processing (especially if there is no further
motivation to pay special attention to new incoming data)
is certainly part of the problem of stereotypes.
  5. My question for the day is this: It seems that the use of the representative hueristic contributes to the continuous use of stereotypes. So when people see someone from a certain group that is unlike their own act in a way that is representative of the stereotypic constructs that they have stored in memory this further validates the stereotypes and their ideas of the group. How do we get people to consider the mistakes that are made when we use these heuristics? Do you think that this will help people to stop overgeneralizing about other groups? This reminds me of the saying "one bad apple spoils the bunch". This topic also corresponds very well with what Myron Rothbart talked about yesterday.
    Yes, modern stereotype research has been influenced quite
a bit by the heuristics-and-biases research and the
congnitive turn in psychology in general.
