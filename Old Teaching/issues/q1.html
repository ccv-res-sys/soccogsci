  1. What is Comte's Pyramid of the Sciences?

    Mathematics -- Astronomy -- Physics -- Chemistry -- Biology --
Sociology. (Psychology is part of both biology and sociology)
This pyramid is hierarchical in that each lower science presupposes the facts
of the higher ones.

  5. In order for Little (1991) to discuss the nature of social explanation, he mentions the meaning of a scientific explanation. However, is it assumed that a scientific explanation may be applied to social phenomena as readily as it is applied to phenomena in the so-called hard sciences? If this is the case, then is this nothing more than a weak attempt of the soft sciences to emulate the hard sciences and to thereby gain the needed credibility within the scientific community?

    Because of the basic assumption of causality, the general form of
explanations applies to social as well as to other phenomena.  (If you
deny that assumption, you might deny the applciability of explanations
as well.)  This general form of explanation subsumes the
explanandum under a general hypothesis/principle/theory/law and thus
describes it as a product of some causes and circumstances.

  7. My question is in regards to the Birnbaum article on the criteria for explanation. It appears that most of the criteria are intuitively correct. However, I do not see that C3 (predictive) and C4 (causal) are necessarily different criteria. If the explanation "contains a causal argument," then does it not meet the "predictive" criteria?

    Predictions do not necessarily include causal mechanisms.
Astrologers and psychics make predictions, too, but they almost never
specify the mechanism that (supposedly) will make the predicitons come
true.  On the other hand, some causal explanations may be "hindsight"
statements that do not lead to any new predictions.  Psychoanalysis is
often (and evolutionary psychology sometimes) accused of producing
such explanations.  One would have to look at them one at a time to
determine whether they are or are not "empirically empty."  The test
question is simple: If the explanation is true, what follows from it?
And how can we test whether what follows from it is actually true?
(This is the explanation-prediction game in which proposed
explanations are tested by deriving predcitions from them and testing
those.)

      Many people would argue that 'number' is a non-empirical

of empirical testability implies that you verify or falsify

    • Levels of explanation/role of function. You seemed to be championing the role of proximate explanation, in for instance, the example of whether one would explain aggression to one's child (or was it grandmother? :)) at the hormonal or behavioral level. Yet the synthesis of approach is not just an abstract, nice-not-but-necessary means for obtaining the elusive Grand Unified Theory of aggression, but an active part of guiding theory at very different levels. If a social psychologist is aware of the physiological research and knows that, for instance, hormone levels fluctuate in response to status challenges, she or he can extend a behavioral, purely-social level theory to a new set of actors for whom there would otherwise not have been any reason to even carve out as a group. Explanation at other levels isn't an aspiration, but (in my humble opinion) a dynamic, and therefore integral, part of the social theorizing process... Along the lines of "carving up the territory", I (unsuccessfully) tried to comment that theories built along the lines of ultimate function could more reliably be "backtranslated" into phenomena, especially as one is usually *asking* an ultimate-functionality question in even pursuing the phenomenon. Take, for example, the instance of a theory of humor -- your example of an unspecifiable theory. A Freudian definition of humor as displaced aggression offers a limited function of humor -- about the same level of function you offered for the "objective self-awareness" theory. At this level, the theory is indeed unspecifiable. At the ultimate level of function, however, a theory of humor would ask what humor gains the individual. Any potential answer (e.g., ability to demonstrate social competence or status) *includes* predictions about the conditions under which humor will be attempted (coalition-building activities, presence of superiors needing to be impressed, threats to the joker's other capacities to demonstrate socially competence..) -- all testable. Not only does this seem to be a serviceable theory of humor, it seems truer to the phenomenon (probably something *like* "why do people use humor?") than a mid-level functionality "theory" that answers the question by defining humor as, i.e., displaced aggression, and then "builds" on it to discover that men, but not women, use humor in crowded rooms, but not elevators, when the data are Winsorized and arcsine transformed...

        I would be very skeptical about "ultimate"
functions.  And I doubt that people are after those
ultimate functions when they try to explain pehnomena.
Sometimes they ask "what for" questions, being interested
in function or purpose; most often they ask "where from"
questions, being interested in origin i.e. cause.  If you
are interested in the function of laughing, you will
provide different answers to why Jones laughed than
someone who is interested in the causes of laughing.  And,
worse, you being interested in the ultimate function of
laughing may give very different answers than someone who
is interested in the functionS of laughing.  As
intentional beings we can adopt almost any (evolved or
not) behavior to fulfill occurrent purposes.  I have the
feeling that evolution's arm doesn't stretch as far as you
may think....
