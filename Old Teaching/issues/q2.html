Lecture 2: History and
main lines of thinking
  1. Zimbardo discusses the "power of the situation." here, he states that behavior in any given interaction is determined less by personality qualities (or what you bring to the interaction) than by the uncontrollable forces of the setting. is this truly an empirical conclusion supported by social psychological research (if so, what research?) and what clinical implications does this have? (even from a folk psychology perspective, isn't consistency in behavior across settings considered "mature?")

  2. It seems that the most powerful aspect of social psychology is its applicability to practical issues in law, education, politics, etc. How does research in social psychology affect public policy? Whose responsibility is it to bridge the gap between research findings and practical application? Is this link effectively made? 
    Let's hope Robert Mauro has a more optimistic answer
than I do...

  3. It is clear from the introductory chapter by Aronson et al. that social psychology studies the psychological processes common to all people that make them susceptible to social influence, but I am curious as to what field of research investigates how what the individual brings to the situation affects how the situation influences him/her. I am thinking not of personality characteristics, but rather past experiences, expectations, goals, and intentions. Is this a topic for social psychologists? Who, if anyone, investigates this question? 
    Most of this is researched by "social cognitive learning 
theorists," especially Albert Bandura, Walter Mischel, and the
latter's most famous student Nancy Cantor.  Lawrence Pervin has 
done quite a bit of work on goals.  These are first leads, but 
there is surely more.

  4. Aronson et al. argue that the behaviorist approach is too simplistic to provide a complete understanding of social behavior. Really? It seems reasonable to view social psychology as a subset of behaviorial psychology. Couldn't the social environment be viewed as a type of reinforcement, positive or negative, for specific behaviors? 
    Nobody would deny the influences of learning and
reinforcement on social behavior.  But many more processes must be
considered, both ones that influence behavior and ones that mediate
the influence of the environment on behavior.

  5. Aronson et al. suggest that two primary motives (need for accuracy and need for high self-regard) underlie thoughts and behavior. First, I recall (years ago) reading something about Swann's notion of self-verification strategies. How is this concept related to accuracy? 
    I say a little bit about that in my paper on negative
self-views (assigned for the Self lecture). Briefly,
self-verification is the "need" to be seen the way one sees
oneself (even if that is negative).  Self-verification and
self-enhancement are needs that sometimes conflict, and the
research suggests that self-verification is stronger on the
cognitive level, whereas self-enhancement is stronger on
the affective level (see the papers by Swann et al).
    Second, could the act of engaging in downward social comparison processes involve the integration of the need for accuracy and the desire to maintain a positive self-view? In other words, if people choose to compare themselves to others who are lower on a given dimension, then they are presumably achieving an accurate perception while also maintaining high self-esteem. If this assertion is correct, then why would anyone choose to engage in upward comparison processes and thereby maintain accuracy at the expense of a positive self-view? Would Taylor's idea concerning positive illusions be applicable to this last question? 
    Interesting and, I think, correct reasoning.  One
could selectively engage in downward comparisons and, with
this biased sample, create locally accurate but overall
unrepresentative information about the self. One could call
it a "positive illusion" or a self-enhancement bias. On the
other point, several people have tried to get a handle on
the motives for downward vs. upward social comparisons.  As
usual, there are many such motives. One reason for upward
comparisons (even if they hurt a little) is to set higher
goals, improve oneself, or to check whether that
improvement has progressed.  Such self-regulation serves
bothe the accuracy and (eventually) the self-enhancement motive.

  6. In the Aronson et al. paper, they chronicle several real-life examples of strange social phenomena. The first one is the most striking for me (the one where four people are killed and another wounded without anyone ever calling the police). I just CAN'T BELIEVE that I would act in the same way as these neighbors. I have been in similar situations before, and every time I have called the police or notified someone. Is it because I have learned about these tendencies in people, and so consciously avoid behaving that way? I know it's more than just the simple fact that there were a bunch of lame neighbors there.
