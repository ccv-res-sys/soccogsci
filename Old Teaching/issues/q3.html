  8. I was wondering, has anyone used cognitive dissonance theory in conjunction with the work on the obedience or conformity to examine what happens to a person after they have obeyed or conformed? Are they more or less likely to obey in the future? Do they change their mind about the situation and justify their behavior by saying that what they did was right under the circumstances?
    Implications for human-on-human atrocities during war?!? 

    Unfortunately you are absolutely right.  Albert Bandura
once pointed out in a talk he gave at Stanford that this
"disengagement" from one's own immoral behavior (denying
the act, justifying it in light of "larger" goods,
dehumanizing the victim, etc.) is one of the biggest
challenges for violence prevention.  Only by
strengthening people's self-standards for moral behavior
(through socialization, rehabilitation, communcal

aggression (anger management, non-violent conflict 
resolution techniques, etc.)
  9. My question of the week deals with the "The Power of the Situation" reading, specifically the theoretical significance of Asch's findings. Several paragraphs (pg. 31-32) are devoted to discussing how Asch did not conclude that perceptions of physical reality can be socially dictated. How is it, then, that these studies are used to support situational influence hypotheses when only a third of their subjects showed a strong situational influence? This seems more like personal differences to me when only a third of the subjects show the effect. Maybe certain people are "weaker" in social situations than others and these people conform more readily to established norms, thus creating the effect.

    A frequency count of a certain behavior (such as
conformity in the Asch situation) shows in some sense
individual differences whenever the frequency is not 0%
or 100%.  But the question is whether, say, a 30:70
split is predictable by any measureable stable trait and
whether the people who show conformity in one situation
are likely to be the ones who show conformity in another
situation. Alternatively, the 30% incidence of
conformity in the Asch situation could be a stable
collective pattern that emerges whenever you run the
experiment, even though we can never predict which
individual will or will not show the behavior.
