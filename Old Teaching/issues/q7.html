Lecture 7: Categorization, stereotypes, and intergroup conflict

  1. A. I was intrigued by the idea, which you pointed out last Thursday, that different areas within psychology seem quite comfortable studying things which other areas won't touch, such as beliefs, desires, and intention. I have also noted that different areas seem to be reluctant to use similar terminology or even to use information from areas which might support or at least provide ideas for research. For that reason, I was particularly delighted to note the comments in the Rothbart et al. article on arbitrary boundaries concerning the parallels between their category work and similar studies and results in perception. My question is, why don't we see more of this? It might be interesting to see how the concept of on-center/off-center receptive fields and inhibition fit into the overall scheme of social categories. Michael Anderson has used it in relation to memory retrieval, and it might apply to spcial groupings as well.
    B. Regarding the historical perspective chapter - why is it that being dissimilar is (apparently) thought of as being worse? Is there some egocentricity at work that is "necessary" for the survival of the individual (in some ecological/evolutionary sense)that causes us humans to label anything different as bad? Could it be an outgrowth/remnant of the needs of mammals in the wild to identify with a particular group in order to survive and reproduce? Also, does this pattern hold true for individuals with negative self schemata? And, is it as true for females as it is for males?

  2. The Rothbart & Lewis article details many theories about what influences intergroup relations. Some influences seem more important than others. This suggests that societal relationships may be influenced by a hierarchy of factors . . . Does this make sense? Has there been any work attempting to rank the relative importance of all these social influences?

  3. My question today comes from one of the quotes by Lippmann. This is terribly small scale, but the more I tried to figure out what he meant, the more difficult it got for me to answer it. Lippmann says "The persistent difficulty is to secure maps on which their own need, or someone else's need, has not sketched in the coast of Bohemia." What does this mean? I looked up Bohemia, and it was originally a kingdom surrounded by mountains (and therefore not having a coast at all). So it could mean that we need maps that do not conjure up an image that doesn't exist in real life (for instance, a map, or stereotype, that attributes qualities to a group that are not accurate). "Bohemian" refers to someone who lives an easy-going middle class life style untroubled by social standards. How would this change the metaphor?

  4. Are there cognitive process theories which explain intragroup violence (e.g., gang violence among individuals of one ethnic group)? Or are some of the causes mentioned (frustration, competition, etc.) applicable still?

  5. In Rothbart's studies he investigates the connection between cognitive processes such as exemplar comparison, and stereotyping. As more and more research is being done on the nature of this link, is anyone investigating what this means for intergroup relations? How can the negative effects of stereotyping (i.e. discrimination) be lessened if our brains are limited to certain forms of thought?

  6. There isn't much of a discussion on the utility of stereotypes. aren't stereotypes useful in several situations? what kind of value judgments do researchers make about this issue?
