Lecture 5: Social Influence

  1. To the extent that Asch had subjects agree or disagree with others on line comparison, his study on conformity is very simple. Conformity outside of the laboratory (the phenomenon) would undoubtedly be much more complex. Is what Asch provides a basic general model for social pressure and conformity? How far can we go with the implications of his study? Certainly, behaviors such as the type of clothing we prefer and the type of hair-do we have are related to more than just the size of the majority, the majority unanimity, and support/desertion of our "partners".
    Asch's experiment demonstrates conformity pressures
on the conscious, deliberative mind. In that sense, it is a
model of opinion formation, judgment in groups (e.g., jury
decisions!), etc.  Many social behaviors, however, conform
to norms without involving such deliberation.  To explain
those behaviors, Asch's experiment does not provide much
guidance.

  2. In the Brownstein & Katzev and Freedman & Fraser articles, I have an ethical worry about public-supported research using deception to get people to give up their scarce resources (money, time). I'd feel better if those people who donated to the art museum got their money back, or if those housewives got paid for their considerable time spent. I would worry less if the participants knew what was going on, but since they were being intentionally deceived about the nature of the research, it bugs me that they also had to give something of value. I assume these experiments passed muster with human subjects committees somewhere, but where should the line be drawn in research that seeks participant compliance without real informed consent?
    There are problems with this approach, but if we want
naturalistic studies, we can't ask for informed consent
every step of the way.  Also think about it this way: The
Portland Art Museum may have been doing a fund drive
anyway; they simply wanted to compare which method was the
best and hired some psychologists to assess the success of
each method.  If it had been a mere experiment, I agree,
the participants should have gotten their money back.

  4. Has research investigated whether there are individual differences in the extent to which a person may be influenced by compliance techniques? For example, would a person with low self-esteem be more likely to be swayed by a compliance technique compared to a person with higher self-esteem? Or, would a person who is unwilling to conform to the majority opinion in a laboratory setting (i.e., in an Asch experiment) be less likely than a conformist in the lab to be swayed by a compliance technique in a field setting? Also, if a person is aware that a request will benefit the requester (say, for example, when encountered by a salesperson), will any compliance technique still work?
    See above, and we talked about the force (and limits)
of awareness in class.  Basically, most "peripheral"
attempts of influence are successful only if the recipient
has low or no awareness of the attempt. One exception is
flattery/compliments/ego-boost---that one works even if
you know it's a tactic.

  6. My question arises from Asch's concluding comments: these findings "raise questions about our ways of education and about the values that guide our conduct." How much is conformity a matter of trait-like tendencies? He seemed to show great individual differences in his results. And if this is indeed a question of our ways of education, are the nonconformists those who have learned something beyond the current curriculum? And lastly, what exactly would we teach?

    See comments above on individual differences.  One
powerful lesson of the Asch, Milgram, and Zimbardo studies

make people perform actions that they would normally never 
perform.  Among other things, we have to teach people to
get rid of their feeling of vulnerability ("I would
never...").

    B. Asch noted that nearly all his subjects agreed that independence is better than conformity, but their behavior clearly did not match this value system. Did the subjects not view their preformance as conformity, or did they perhaps SAY what the group said while internally maintaining their own different belief? If the latter is true, then the external behavior was conforming, but the internal belief was independent. What difference does that make?
    Yes, the interpretation is that most people still believed that their
perception was correct, but they succumbed to the group pressure on the
level of verbal reports.  The crucial implication is that jury
members, cabinet members, and other important decision makers may
succumb to social pressures as well and go with the majority opinion even
if it is dead wrong (see research on "groupthink").
