  4. Reading the papers on self-concept started me thinking about how some people take strikingly similar situations and internalize them totally differently, giving the same situations either positive or negative associations (this is an extreme example). As I understand, the papers suggest that the tightness of the self-concept of negative-self-viewers makes the negative affect more readily retrievable than it is for a positive-self-viewer. I was wondering, first, what causes the initial construction of the concepts? And then, to what extent do you think chemicals in the brain, such as neurotransmitters, affect this construction? From novice observation, it seems to me that some people are just "happier" in general than others, and perhaps this long term affect (mood) is due to raised levels of "positive affect" NTs in the brain, maybe opiates or something. (I mean this as opposed to the results of a LACK of certain chemicals as in certain types of depressed patients.) Would the result of this chemical make-up over years of development foster the construction of a positive self-concept, somewhat independent of experience? To what extent would you guess the presence of certain neurotransmitters affects selp-concepts? 
    The discussion section of Malle & Horowitz (1995)
outlines some (psychological) mechanisms that may lead to
tight negative schemata.  I have not thought much about
the influence of generalized self-esteem on the
development of domain-specific self-views nor about the
neurophysiological basis of such generalized self-esteem.
Mary Rothbart's work on the neurocognitive basis of
temperament may be relevant here.

  5. Based on personal observation, it seems as if there are significant gender differences in self-views. If this observation is true, then can it be assumed that women maintain their self-worth in the face of threat in a manner different from men? Also, could the discrepancy between one's ideal self and actual self be greater for women than for men? Moreover, research seems to suggest that more women suffer from depression than men. Thus, if gender differences in self-views do exist, and if women are more prone to depression, could this suggest that women have more tightly organized negative self-views compared to men? 
    Very interesting hypotheses that have not, as far as
I know, been tested.  It would be fascinating to link
vulnerability to negative self-schemas with vulnerability
to depression.  I do not remember consistent sex
differences in my data reported in Malle & Horowitz
(1995), but we did not look for them specifically.  Here
is an important project to be planned out!

  6. In your overview paper on the self you state early on that "dissonance between ongoing behavior and beliefs MUST be assessed in a working self-concept that represents all self-relevant states at a given time and assesses their consistency." I have trouble with this statement. The term "working self-concept" suggests a basically conscious awareness of the self. And as you say yourself, a large part of the dissonance reduction process is unconscious. Why must dissonance be assessed in a working self-concept? Am I missing something??? 
    The key is that the working self-concept need to be fully
conscious either; it can be "preconscious"---that is,
easily made conscious with the right kind of question.

  7. How can we be confident that negative self-views are not confounded by mood? is a negative self-view associated with an acute or chronic (or both) definition of the self? 
    Depends on what you mean by "confounded."  The
consistency effects reported in Malle & Horowitz (1995),
for example, cannot be explained by mood (as pointed out
in the discussion section of the paper).  But of course,
people with a negative self-view are more likely to feel
negative affect/mood when that self-view is activated.
Also, the more domain-specific negative self-views you
have, the greater the likelihood that you will be more
broadly "depressed."

  8. This question is a reaction to the article on negative self-views. It was suggested in the article that people with negative self-views produce more consistent self-descriptions, and that research indicates that the presence of negative thoughts better predict behaviors than the presence of positive thoughts. Could it be that psychologists and researchers have traditionally primarily examined negative behaviors, thus being more successful at measuring such behaviors?
    That's an interesting possibility.  Only a
