  1. My questions are related to the social cognition and social perception reading. How can we prevent our stereotypic constructs from guiding our interpretations and reactions to situations? Doesn't this problem have strong negative implications for eye witness testimony? Higgins & Bargh state that studies have shown that social judgments are not automatically made during the acquisition of information but depend on the subject having the goal of making them. Can the automatic activation of stereotypic constructs effect the likelihood of making a social judgment when a person doesn't even have a goal of making one? 
     There is some recent evidence in the literature
(since this article was published) that automatic
activation does exist: when primed with a category label,
for example, stereotypic attributes associated with that
label are activated.  Typically, studies show that an
attribute is activated by demonstrating faster reaction
times for some related lexical decision.  But making a
judgment is different from (often more conscious and
deliberated than) being activated, so the dispute
over this issue will continue.
  2. After reading your "Intentinality and Explanation" talk, this question came to mind: At what point does the misinterpretation of intention come into play? Can just one of the 5 factors (desire, beliefs, intention, skill, awareness) be misinferred? Or must it be a combination of the 5? This would be an interesting question to empirically test, and I am curious if it has already been done.
    There are three types of misinterpretation we have to
distinguish: (a) judging a behavior intentional even though it is
actually unintentional; (b) judging a behavior unintentional
even though it is actually intentional; (c) inferring the
wrong purpose/goal from an intentional behavior.  For (a),
there are many possibilities of misinference.  The actual
unintentional behavior may have 0 to 4 of the 5 components
present and still be unintentional, and the inference may
therefore misinfer anywhere between 1 and 5 components.
For (b),  the actually intentional behavior must have all
5 components present and the misjudgment can err on any 1
or more of the components.  For (c), if the behavior is
identified correctly and judged intentional, the misjudgment
usually focusses on the desire (purpose, goal) component:

  4. Heider's theory of "Naive Psychology", or "Folk theory," states that person perception, like object perception, is based on several factors. Does the theory allow for consideration of individual differences, such that for some people the personal characteristics may play a larger role in determining how they interpret the behaviors of others? For example, a paranoid individual is likely to interpret threats in most any situations. The situations need not be threatenting (in "reality").
    There is certainly a lot of room for individual
differences.  Some research shows that the (over)readiness
to infer hostile intent in others is a crucial predictor
of the perceiver's own aggressive (retaliatory) behavior
(see here for some recent references)
