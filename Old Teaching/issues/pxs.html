Excerpt from:

The Person and the Situation:
Conceptual Issues in Theories of Social Behavior

Bertram F. Malle

The Debate

The person-situation controversy has bristled between social and personality psychology for the last 25 years. One of its historical roots is Kurt Lewin's (1935) seemingly innocuous formula, b = f (p, s), expressing that behavior is a function of the person and the situation. Lewin's intent was to integrate behaviorist and psychoanalytic accounts of human behavior. The modern controversy, however, has centered on a more divisive question, namely, the relative contributions of person and situation factors in determining human behavior.

Early studies seemed to undermine the relative importance of personality (e.g., Hartshorne & May, 1928; Lewin, Lippitt, & White, 1939; Newcomb, 1929), but what truly inflamed the controversy was Mischel's (1968) book, Personality and Assessment. Mischel reviewed the literature on the cross-situational consistency of behavior and concluded that it was so low as to call into question the alleged causal role of broad personality traits in determining behavior. For example, the consistency between children's honesty in one social situation (e.g., one's willingness to steal some change left on the table of an empty classroom) and their honesty in a different social situation (e.g., willingness to cheat during an exam) amounted to a correlation of .20. That is, knowing a child's behavior in one situation (whether it was honest or dishonest) gives us no more than a probability of .6 for correctly predicting that child's behavior in the next relevant situation (with .5 being the chance rate for a dichotomous prediction).[1] Low cross-situational consistency does not disprove, of course, the causal role of broad traits, but it seems to leave little hope for their explanatory usefulness.

Two Challenges of Situationism

Data of this sort, pointing to the low cross-situational consistency of personality traits, constitute the first situationist challenge to the power of person factors in accounting for human behavior. Personality traits are assumed to be so weak that "in most [...] novel situations, one cannot predict with any accuracy how particular people will respond." (Ross & Nisbett, 1991, p. 2).

The second challenge, which is the complement of the first, emphasizes the power of situational factors in determining behavior: "What has been demonstrated through a host of celebrated laboratory and field studies is that manipulations of the immediate social situation can overwhelm in importance the type of individual differences in personal traits or dispositions that people normally think of as being determinative of social behavior." (Ross & Nisbett, 1991, p. xiv) The celebrations were probably limited to social psychologists, and direct comparisons between person forces and the overwhelming situation forces have been scarce. Nevertheless, the main point appears valid: One can find impressive studies on the power of situations, such as the following.

Researchers Darley and Batson (1973) asked students of the Princeton Theological Seminary to prepare themselves for a brief talk on the parable of the Good Samaritan, which they were to give in a nearby building. One by one, the experimenter gave students directions to the building and then applied the experimental manipulation by telling the student either that he or she was late for the upcoming talk ("they were expecting you a few minutes ago") or early ("it will be a few minutes before they will be ready for you"). In either case, on the way to the building, there was a man slumped in a doorway, coughing and groaning (who was, of course, a disguised confederate of the experimenter). The study's main question was how many students would spontaneously help that man. The results reflect the strong influence of the manipulated situational factor: Of the theology students who thought they were early, 63% helped the man; of those who thought they were late, only 10% were good Samaritans.

The potency of situational forces does not make the social scientist's life easy, however, as those powerful forces are subtly intertwined. What would happen, for example, if the experimenter told each student that he was selected to give the speech because he was found to be the most altruistic student of his class? Perhaps such a labeling manipulation (Miller, Brickman, & Bolen, 1975) would reverse the classic pattern, making those who are late for the speech most likely to help because a prosocial act under time pressure would be perceived as particularly diagnostic of one's altruistic character (cf. Frank, 1988). Which situational factors are effective and which aren't is typically unknown until after the fact because "situational effects can sometimes be far different from what our intuitions, or theories, or even the existing psychological literature tell us they should be" (Ross & Nisbett, 1991, p. 6). In less charitable vernacular, "We haven't the foggiest idea why that did or didn't work (Goldberg, 1992, p. 90).

The power of the situation is further complicated by the fact that different people perceive the same objective situation differently (Ross, 1990). This phenomenon has been documented widely, using stimuli such as football games (Hastorf & Cantril, 1954), research articles (Lord, Lepper, & Ross, 1979), and people (e.g., Kelley, 1952). The subjective interpretation of a situation is a powerful determinant of behavior over and above the objective situation itself. Does this subjectivism open a new door, then, for the power of personality traits? Perhaps, but the hard-core situationist might still insist that an actor's subjective interpretation of a situation is not a stable disposition. Future research must address this issue.

Two clarifications are in order. First, most situationists agree that the consistency (in reality and in the laboratory) of ability dispositions (e.g., intelligence, skills) is considerably large (cf. Mischel, 1968), so the situationist challenge is limited to personality dispositions. Second, most situationists do not deny the reality of behavioral consistency in everyday life, but they ascribe such consistency to individually constraining social forces (e.g., people's roles and the expectations of others) rather than to the influence of personality dispositions. Once these constraining forces are eliminated, as in most psychological experiments, the pure consistency due to dispositions drops to the familiar coefficient of .20.

The Reaction

Many personality psychologists were disturbed by these challenges, and some were even enraged because they felt it was an invalid attack against a whole discipline. Even though the attack originated in a heresy committed by personologist Mischel, the social camp quickly chimed in. Curiously, social psychologists seemed to gain confidence from the attack--confidence they needed, perhaps, for soothing the wounds inflicted by external and internal derogations of their own field.[2] The impartial observer (if there is one) might suspect that the attack on personality psychology successfully distracted social psychologists from their own self-doubts. Personality psychologists, for their part, failed to find an even weaker scapegoat, so they had to defend themselves against the charges.

I will devote only a brief discussion to the various defenses that ensued. For the most part, they have focused on methodological rather than conceptual issues (see Kenrick & Funder, 1988; Pervin, 1985).

One set of defenses directly addressed the studies that provoked the controversy. Most studies on behavioral consistency, the defenders said, were methodologically flawed, so the behavior-behavior correlations were underestimated. Moreover, assessments of single behaviors are notoriously unreliable (because any single behavior is determined by multiple causes), so the serious limits of behavioral prediction should not be surprising. If behaviors are aggregated, however, predictions can be substantial (Epstein, 1979). For example, if any 2 out of 20 behaviors relevant to honesty show an average intercorrelation of .20, then an aggregate of, say, the first 10 behaviors predicts an aggregate of the second set of 10 behaviors with r = .71 (using the Spearman-Brown formula). Making a dichotomous prediction, we will be correct about 86% of the time, demonstrating a 71% improvement over chance.[3],

Another set of defenses criticized several aspects of the comparison between studies that demonstrated the influence of person factors and those that demonstrated the influence of situation factors. Funder and Ozer (1983) claimed that most classic studies in the social tradition showed effect sizes (i.e., degree of predictability) no higher than those of personality studies. Other authors (e.g., Buss, 1989) pointed out that psychologists can easily create situations in which personality influences are minimized (e.g., under strong norm constraints), and other situations in which personality influences are maximized (e.g., in unstructured interactions). This defense seems to backfire a bit, however, as it implies that the relative predictive power of the situation versus that of the person depends itself on the situation.

Numerous studies tried to demonstrate that this relative predictive power also depends on the person: Some people, on some traits, may be more consistent than others (Bem & Allen, 1974). For example, the personality dispositions that a person values very highly or those that are integral to her goals in life will influence her behavior more markedly than other dispositions. Curiously, this promising idea has been difficult to prove; so far, the predictive gain of such strategies has been rather limited (see Koestner, Bernieri, & Zuckerman, 1989).

How did the situationists respond to these defenses? The few who did remained smug, perhaps because the agitation within personality psychology seemed to indicate that the attack had been on target. Most importantly, social psychologists insisted that personality predictions, even methodologically flawless ones, will always be lower than people expect them to be (Ross & Nisbett, 1991, 1992). Indeed, that personality is predictable, but less than we think, remains an important message--to the stern father, who should not be surprised that his daughter is shy and stuck-up at home but loud and exuberant with her friends at the mall; or to the personnel officer, who should not be surprised that her favorite candidate turned out to be far less responsible, extraverted, and friendly than he appeared in a 20-minute interview.

[In the rest of the paper I turn to an analysis of the conceptual issues that have lurked beneath this controversy but were never made explicit. I distinguish betwen general person factors and individual difference factors and discuss the narrow conflict between individual difference factors and situation factors and then the broader conflict between general person factors and situation factors. I try to show that these three factors each play different roles in psychological theory and that any comprehensive theory must integrate all three factors.]
